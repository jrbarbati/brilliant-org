Every computer you have ever used, from the phone in your pocket to the largest machine in a data center, rests on a small number of simple ideas. This first section of the book is about those ideas. We will ask what computation actually is, how information can be measured, how numbers and text and pictures can be stored as patterns of bits, and how a handful of logic gates can be wired together to add numbers and remember values. By the end you should be able to see a modern computer as a tall stack of layers, each one built out of the layer beneath it, with transistors at the bottom and software at the top.

Let us begin with the word computation itself. A computation is a process that takes some input, follows a fixed sequence of steps, and produces some output. The steps must be so precise that no judgment is needed to carry them out. A recipe is not quite a computation, because a recipe says things like season to taste. A long division problem worked out on paper is a computation, because every step is completely determined by the digits in front of you. The person doing the long division is acting as a machine. In fact, before electronic machines existed, the word computer referred to a human being whose job was to carry out calculations by hand.

The sequence of precise steps is called an algorithm. An algorithm is a finite list of instructions that, when followed exactly, solves a problem for every valid input. The idea of an algorithm is older than any machine. The ancient method for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers is an algorithm. The method you learned in school for multiplying two multi-digit numbers is an algorithm. What the computer adds is speed and reliability: a machine can carry out billions of simple steps every second without getting tired or bored, and it will follow the instructions exactly as written, including any mistakes.

So a computer is a machine that executes algorithms. To make that idea concrete, we need to answer three questions. First, what does the machine operate on? Second, what are the simplest steps it can take? Third, how do we tell it which steps to take? The answers are information, logic, and programs, and we will take them in turn.

Information is the raw material of computation. The surprising thing is that information can be measured, and the unit of measurement is the bit. A bit is the answer to a single yes or no question. If I tell you that a coin landed heads, I have given you one bit of information, because before I spoke there were two equally likely possibilities and now there is one. If I tell you which of four doors a prize is behind, I have given you two bits, because you could have found the same answer by asking two yes or no questions: is it behind one of the first two doors, and then is it behind the first of the remaining pair.

The pattern continues. Eight doors need three bits. Sixteen doors need four bits. In general, n bits can distinguish among two to the power of n possibilities, and to distinguish among m possibilities you need about log base two of m bits. This is the reason that powers of two appear everywhere in computing. A group of 8 bits is called a byte, and a byte can take two to the power of eight different values, which is 256. A thousand twenty four bytes is called a kilobyte, roughly a million bytes is a megabyte, and roughly a billion bytes is a gigabyte.

Why do computers use bits rather than, say, decimal digits? The answer is physical. Inside a computer a bit is represented by a physical quantity with two easily distinguished states, such as a high voltage and a low voltage, a charged capacitor and an empty one, or a magnetic region pointing one way or the other. Two states are the easiest thing in the world to tell apart reliably. If we tried to store ten different voltage levels in one wire, small amounts of electrical noise could push one level into another and corrupt the value. With only two states, there is a wide safety margin, and the machine can run for years without a single bit flipping by accident. Everything else in the computer is built on top of this one reliable distinction.

Since every bit is just a zero or a one, we need a way to represent ordinary numbers using only those two symbols. This is the binary number system, and it works exactly like the decimal system you already know, with one change. In decimal, each position in a number is worth ten times the position to its right. The number three hundred forty two means three hundreds, four tens, and two ones. In binary, each position is worth two times the position to its right. The rightmost position is worth one, the next is worth two, then four, then eight, then sixteen, and so on.

So the binary number one zero one one means one eight, zero fours, one two, and one one, which adds up to eleven. The binary number one one one one is eight plus four plus two plus one, which is fifteen, the largest value that fits in four bits. With 8 bits the largest value is two hundred fifty five, with sixteen bits it is sixty five thousand five hundred thirty five, and with thirty two bits it is a little over four billion. These sizes come up constantly in practice, because computers handle numbers in fixed-width groups of bits.

Converting from binary to decimal is therefore just a matter of adding up the place values of the positions that hold a one. Converting from decimal to binary is the reverse process, and there is a simple algorithm for it. Repeatedly divide the decimal number by two and write down the remainder each time. The remainders, read from last to first, are the binary digits. Take the number thirteen. Thirteen divided by two is six with remainder one. Six divided by two is three with remainder zero. Three divided by two is one with remainder one. One divided by two is zero with remainder one. Reading the remainders backward gives one one zero one, and indeed eight plus four plus one is thirteen.

Another way to convert is to subtract powers of two from the largest downward. To convert forty five, notice that thirty two fits, leaving thirteen. Sixteen does not fit, so write a zero. Eight fits, leaving five. Four fits, leaving one. Two does not fit. One fits, leaving zero. The result is one zero one one zero one. Either method works, and with a little practice you can convert small numbers in your head.

Binary numbers are long, and people find them hard to read. For that reason programmers often write values in base sixteen, called hexadecimal. In hexadecimal the digits run from zero to nine and then continue with the letters a through f standing for ten through fifteen. The attraction is that one hexadecimal digit corresponds to exactly four bits, so a byte is always exactly two hexadecimal digits. The byte one one one one zero zero zero zero becomes f zero in hexadecimal. Base eight, called octal, plays a similar role with groups of three bits. These bases are simply convenient shorthand; the machine itself only ever stores bits.

The binary system as described so far handles only nonnegative whole numbers. Real programs need negative numbers too. The most obvious idea is to reserve one bit as a sign, with zero meaning positive and one meaning negative, and use the remaining bits for the magnitude. This is called sign and magnitude, and it has two awkward problems. It gives two different patterns for zero, a positive zero and a negative zero, and it makes addition complicated, because the machine must inspect the signs and decide whether to add or subtract the magnitudes.

The scheme that almost every computer actually uses is called two's complement. In two's complement with n bits, the leftmost bit has a negative place value: instead of being worth plus two to the power of n minus one, it is worth minus two to the power of n minus one. All other bits keep their ordinary positive place values. With 8 bits, the leftmost bit is worth minus one hundred twenty eight, and the other seven bits are worth sixty four, thirty two, sixteen, eight, four, two, and one. The pattern one zero zero zero zero zero zero zero is therefore minus one hundred twenty eight, the pattern one one one one one one one one is minus one hundred twenty eight plus one hundred twenty seven, which is minus one, and the pattern zero one one one one one one one is positive one hundred twenty seven.

So an 8 bit two's complement number ranges from minus one hundred twenty eight up to positive one hundred twenty seven, and there is exactly one pattern for zero. Even better, ordinary binary addition works unchanged. To add minus one and positive one, add one one one one one one one one to zero zero zero zero zero zero zero one. The result is one followed by eight zeros, but we only keep 8 bits, so the leading one falls off and we are left with zero, which is correct. The machine does not need to know whether the numbers are signed or unsigned; the same adder circuit produces the right bit pattern either way.

There is a quick recipe for negating a two's complement number. Flip every bit, then add one. To find the representation of minus five, start with positive five, which is zero zero zero zero zero one zero one. Flip every bit to get one one one one one zero one zero. Add one to get one one one one one zero one one. Check: minus one hundred twenty eight plus sixty four plus thirty two plus sixteen plus eight plus two plus one is minus five. Applying the same recipe to minus five gets you back to positive five, which is a good sign that the system is consistent.

Fixed-width numbers have a limitation that every programmer eventually runs into. If you add one to the largest value that fits, the result wraps around to the smallest value. With 8 bit two's complement, positive one hundred twenty seven plus one gives minus one hundred twenty eight. This is called overflow, and it has caused real failures in real systems, from video games where the score suddenly went negative to a rocket whose guidance computer converted a large floating point value into a sixteen bit integer that could not hold it. Understanding representation is not merely academic; it is the difference between a program that works and one that fails mysteriously.

Numbers are only the beginning. A computer also has to store text, and the idea here is simple: assign every character a number, and store the number. The oldest widely used assignment is called ASCII, which uses seven bits and therefore has room for one hundred twenty eight characters. In ASCII the capital letter A is sixty five, capital B is sixty six, lowercase a is ninety seven, the digit character zero is forty eight, and the space is thirty two. There are also control characters for things like line breaks and tabs. Because the letters are assigned consecutive values, you can convert a capital letter to lowercase by adding thirty two, and you can sort words alphabetically by comparing their numeric codes.

ASCII was designed for English and has no room for accented letters, let alone the thousands of characters used in Chinese, Japanese, Arabic, or Hindi. The modern solution is Unicode, which assigns a unique number, called a code point, to well over one hundred thousand characters from every writing system in the world, along with symbols and emoji. Unicode code points are usually stored using an encoding called UTF-8, which represents the first one hundred twenty eight code points in a single byte, exactly as ASCII does, and uses two, three, or four bytes for everything else. A text file is nothing more than a long sequence of bytes, and a program that displays the text must know which encoding was used to interpret those bytes as characters.

The lesson generalizes. Bits have no inherent meaning. The byte with value sixty five is the letter A if a program treats it as text, the number sixty five if a program treats it as an integer, part of an instruction if a program treats it as code, and a shade of gray if a program treats it as part of an image. Meaning comes from interpretation, and interpretation is decided by the program. This is one of the most important ideas in all of computing.

Images illustrate the same principle. A digital image is a rectangular grid of tiny squares called pixels, and each pixel holds a number or a small group of numbers describing its color. In a grayscale image, each pixel is a single byte, where zero means black, two hundred fifty five means white, and the values in between are shades of gray. In a color image, each pixel usually holds three bytes, one for the intensity of red, one for green, and one for blue. Mixing those three primary lights in different amounts produces every color on the screen. Three bytes per pixel allow two to the power of twenty four possible colors, which is about sixteen million.

An image that is one thousand pixels wide and one thousand pixels tall therefore has a million pixels and needs about three million bytes when stored naively. That is why image file formats use compression, which is a way of finding patterns in the data and describing them more briefly. Sound works the same way: a microphone measures air pressure thousands of times per second, and each measurement is stored as a number. Video is simply a sequence of images shown quickly, accompanied by sound. Every kind of data you have ever seen on a screen is, underneath, a long row of bits, and the machine is happy to treat all of it with the same small set of operations.

We now know what a computer operates on. The next question is what the simplest steps are. Since every value is a pattern of bits, the simplest possible operations are ones that take one or two bits as input and produce one bit as output. The study of these operations is called boolean algebra, after the nineteenth century mathematician George Boole, who worked out the rules of logic as a kind of arithmetic long before any machine could use them. In boolean algebra there are only two values, true and false, which we will write as one and zero.

The first operation is NOT. NOT takes a single bit and flips it. NOT of one is zero, and NOT of zero is one. The second operation is AND. AND takes two bits and produces one only if both inputs are one. The third is OR, which produces one if at least one input is one. The fourth is XOR, short for exclusive or, which produces one if exactly one of the inputs is one, and zero if the inputs are the same. You can think of AND as asking whether both conditions hold, OR as asking whether either holds, and XOR as asking whether the two inputs differ.

The clearest way to define such an operation is with a truth table, which simply lists every possible combination of inputs and the resulting output. For a two input operation there are four rows, since each of the two inputs can be zero or one. The truth table for AND reads: zero and zero gives zero; zero and one gives zero; one and zero gives zero; one and one gives one. The truth table for OR reads: zero or zero gives zero; zero or one gives one; one or zero gives one; one or one gives one. The truth table for XOR reads: zero xor zero gives zero; zero xor one gives one; one xor zero gives one; one xor one gives zero.

Truth tables are useful because they turn questions about logic into questions about tables, and tables can be checked mechanically. Two expressions are equivalent if and only if their truth tables match on every row. This lets you prove laws of boolean algebra by exhaustion. For example, NOT of x AND y is the same as NOT x OR NOT y, and NOT of x OR y is the same as NOT x AND NOT y. These two facts are called De Morgan's laws, and you can verify them by writing out four rows each. Other useful laws are that x AND one is x, x OR zero is x, x AND zero is zero, x OR one is one, x AND x is x, and x XOR x is zero.

A remarkable fact is that every possible function from bits to bits, no matter how many inputs it has or how complicated it looks, can be built out of AND, OR, and NOT. Here is why. Write out the truth table of the function. For every row where the output is one, build an AND expression that is true for exactly that combination of inputs, using NOT on the inputs that should be zero. Then join all those AND expressions with OR. The resulting expression produces one on precisely the rows you wanted. Going further, it turns out that a single operation called NAND, which is NOT of AND, is enough by itself, because NOT, AND, and OR can each be built from NAND alone. This means a factory only needs to know how to manufacture one kind of building block.

That building block, in physical form, is the logic gate. A logic gate is a small electronic circuit whose input wires and output wire carry voltages representing zero and one, and whose output voltage is determined by its inputs exactly according to a truth table. An AND gate outputs high voltage only when both inputs are high. An OR gate outputs high when either input is high. A NOT gate, often called an inverter, outputs high when its input is low. Gates are built from transistors, which are tiny switches made of silicon that allow a voltage on one terminal to control whether current flows between two others. A NOT gate can be made from two transistors, and a NAND gate from four. A modern processor contains billions of transistors, which means billions of gates, all switching billions of times per second.

What makes gates powerful is that they can be connected. The output of one gate can feed the input of another, and the result is a circuit that computes a more complicated function. We will now build two of the most important circuits in any computer, an adder and a memory cell, from nothing but gates. If you can follow these two constructions, you have understood the essential trick by which logic becomes computation.

Start with addition of two single bits. There are four cases. Zero plus zero is zero. Zero plus one is one. One plus zero is one. One plus one is two, which in binary is one zero, meaning a sum bit of zero and a carry bit of one. Look at the sum bit on its own: it is one exactly when the inputs differ, which is the truth table of XOR. Look at the carry bit on its own: it is one exactly when both inputs are one, which is the truth table of AND. So a circuit with one XOR gate and one AND gate, both fed by the same two inputs, adds two bits and produces both the sum and the carry. This circuit is called a half adder.

To add numbers longer than one bit, we add column by column from right to left, exactly as on paper, and each column may receive a carry from the column to its right. So each column needs a circuit with three inputs, the two bits being added and the incoming carry, and two outputs, the sum bit and the outgoing carry. This is called a full adder, and it can be built from two half adders and an OR gate. The first half adder adds the two input bits. The second half adder adds that result to the incoming carry. The outgoing carry is one if either half adder produced a carry, which is what the OR gate computes.

Now chain full adders together. To add two 8 bit numbers, line up eight full adders, feed each one the corresponding pair of bits, connect the carry out of each adder to the carry in of the next adder to its left, and set the first carry in to zero. The eight sum outputs are the 8 bit result, and the final carry out tells you whether overflow occurred. This circuit is called a ripple carry adder, because the carry ripples from right to left through the chain. It is slow for wide numbers, and real processors use cleverer designs that compute carries in parallel, but the principle is the same. A machine that can add can also subtract, by negating one input with the two's complement recipe, and with a little more circuitry it can multiply and compare. All of arithmetic grows from one XOR gate and one AND gate.

The adder is a combinational circuit, meaning its output depends only on its current inputs. Change the inputs and the output changes immediately; there is no memory of the past. But a useful computer must also remember things, and this requires a different trick. The trick is feedback: wiring the output of a circuit back around to its own input. Consider two NOT gates connected in a loop, with the output of the first feeding the input of the second and the output of the second feeding the input of the first. If the first output is one, the second output is zero, which keeps the first output at one. If the first output is zero, the second output is one, which keeps the first output at zero. The loop has two stable states and will hold either one indefinitely. It is a one bit memory.

The trouble with the two inverter loop is that there is no way to change what it holds. Replace the two NOT gates with two NOR gates, where NOR means NOT of OR, and give each gate a second input. Now one of those extra inputs can be used to force the loop into the one state, and the other can be used to force it into the zero state. When both extra inputs are released back to zero, the loop remembers whichever state it was last forced into. This circuit is called a latch, and more elaborate versions called flip-flops are designed to change state only at the instant a clock signal ticks, so that all the memory in a machine updates in lockstep.

A single flip-flop holds one bit. A row of eight flip-flops holds a byte, and such a row is called a register. A processor contains a few dozen registers that hold the values it is actively working on. Main memory, the gigabytes of RAM in a computer, is organized differently for reasons of cost and density, usually as an enormous grid of tiny capacitors that must be refreshed many times per second, but the logical picture is the same. Memory is a long numbered list of storage locations, each holding a fixed number of bits, and each location has an address. To read a value, the processor sends the address to the memory and receives the bits stored there. To write a value, it sends the address and the new bits.

We now have the two essential components. An arithmetic circuit built from gates can compute functions of bits, and a memory circuit built from gates can hold bits over time. The remaining question is how to organize these components into a machine that can execute any algorithm we choose, rather than a single fixed function wired in at the factory. The answer that has dominated computer design since the nineteen forties is called the von Neumann architecture, after the mathematician John von Neumann, who wrote an influential description of it, though the ideas were developed by a group of engineers working together.

The von Neumann architecture has four parts. The first is the memory, which we just described. The second is the processing unit, which contains the arithmetic and logic circuits along with the registers. The third is the control unit, which decides what the processing unit should do next. The fourth is the input and output system, which connects the machine to keyboards, screens, disks, and networks. The processing unit and control unit together are called the central processing unit, or CPU.

The single most important idea in this architecture is that the program lives in the same memory as the data. Earlier machines were programmed by physically rewiring them or by setting rows of switches. In a von Neumann machine, each instruction of the program is encoded as a pattern of bits, exactly like a number, and stored in memory. The control unit keeps a special register called the program counter, which holds the address of the next instruction. The machine runs in an endless loop with three steps. First, fetch the instruction at the address in the program counter. Second, decode the instruction to figure out what operation it requests and which registers or memory locations it involves. Third, execute the operation, and then advance the program counter to the next instruction. This is called the fetch decode execute cycle, and it runs billions of times per second in a modern processor.

Because instructions are just bit patterns in memory, a program can be loaded from a disk, sent over a network, or even generated by another program. The same physical machine can run a spreadsheet one moment and a video game the next, simply by pointing the program counter at a different region of memory. This flexibility is what makes a computer a general purpose machine rather than a calculator.

So what is a program, concretely? At the lowest level, a program is a list of machine instructions, each one a pattern of bits that the control unit knows how to decode. A typical instruction might say: load the value at memory address five hundred into register one. Another might say: add register one and register two and put the result in register three. Another might say: if register three is zero, set the program counter to address two hundred, otherwise continue. That last kind of instruction, called a conditional branch, is what makes the machine capable of making decisions and repeating steps. With loads, stores, arithmetic, and conditional branches, a machine can execute any algorithm that can be written down at all.

Writing programs directly as bit patterns is painful, so very early on people invented assembly language, in which each instruction is written as a short word plus its operands, and a program called an assembler translates the words into bits. Assembly is still tedious, because each line does only one tiny thing. The next step was the high level programming language, in which a single line can express an idea like add up all the values in this list, and a program called a compiler translates it into many machine instructions. Languages like C, Python, and Java sit at this level. The programmer writes in terms of variables, loops, and functions, and never thinks about registers or addresses unless there is a reason to.

This brings us to the idea that ties the whole section together: abstraction. An abstraction is a simplified view of something that hides the details you do not need and exposes only the ones you do. A computer is built as a tower of abstractions, and each layer in the tower is built from the layer beneath it while hiding how that layer works. At the bottom are physical devices, transistors made of silicon, whose behavior is a matter of physics and chemistry. The next layer treats a transistor as an ideal switch, ignoring the messy details of how electrons move. The next layer wires switches into logic gates and thinks only in terms of truth tables. The next layer wires gates into adders, registers, and memory, and thinks in terms of bits and arithmetic. The next layer assembles those components into a processor with an instruction set, and thinks in terms of fetch, decode, and execute.

Above the processor sits the operating system, a program that manages memory, schedules other programs, and offers services like reading a file or drawing a window. Above that sit programming languages and their compilers, which let a programmer think in terms of variables and functions instead of registers and addresses. Above that sit libraries and frameworks, and above those sit the applications a person actually uses. Each layer offers a clean interface to the layer above and depends only on the interface of the layer below. A web developer does not need to know how a NAND gate is built, and a chip designer does not need to know how a web page is laid out. The tower is so tall that no single person understands every level in full detail, and yet the whole thing works because the interfaces between layers are precise.

Abstraction is also the reason the same program can run on very different hardware. A program written in a high level language can be compiled for a phone, a laptop, or a server, because the language abstracts away the differences between their processors. Likewise the same processor design can be manufactured with different transistor technologies, because the gate level abstraction hides the physics. When an engineer improves one layer without changing its interface, every layer above benefits for free. This is how computers have become millions of times faster over a few decades while old programs continue to run.

Let us review the main ideas before moving on. Computation is the mechanical execution of an algorithm, a precise sequence of steps that transforms input into output. Information is measured in bits, and n bits can distinguish among two to the power of n possibilities. Binary numbers use place values that are powers of two, and converting between bases is a matter of repeated division or repeated subtraction. Two's complement gives the leftmost bit a negative place value so that negative numbers can be added with the same circuit as positive ones. Text is stored by assigning a number to each character, and images are stored as grids of numbered pixels, so that every kind of data is a pattern of bits whose meaning is fixed by interpretation.

Boolean algebra provides the operations AND, OR, NOT, and XOR, each defined by a truth table, and every function of bits can be built from these. Logic gates are physical circuits that implement these operations with transistors. A half adder is one XOR gate and one AND gate, a full adder is two half adders and an OR gate, and a chain of full adders adds whole numbers. Feedback between gates creates a circuit with stable states, which is a memory cell, and rows of memory cells form registers and main memory. The von Neumann architecture stores the program in memory alongside the data and runs a fetch decode execute cycle, which makes one machine capable of running any program. Finally, the entire structure is organized as layers of abstraction, from transistors through gates and processors to operating systems, languages, and applications.

In the sections that follow we will climb this tower. We will look more closely at algorithms and at how to measure how much work they do, at the data structures that organize information in memory, at the theory of what can and cannot be computed at all, and eventually at the learning systems that let a machine improve its own behavior from data. Every one of those topics rests on the foundation laid here. Whenever something later in the book seems mysterious, it can help to come back and remember that beneath everything there is nothing but bits, gates, and a very fast loop of fetch, decode, and execute.


In the first section of this book we looked at how a computer stores information as bits and how a machine can be built to move those bits around. In this section we turn from the machine itself to the instructions we give it. The instructions that tell a computer how to solve a problem are called an algorithm, and algorithms are the heart of computer science. A computer without an algorithm is like a kitchen without a recipe; the tools are all there, but nothing useful happens until someone writes down the steps.

An algorithm is a finite list of precise steps that takes some input and produces some output. That is the whole definition, and it is worth reading twice because every word in it carries weight. The steps must be finite, which means the list of instructions has an end and does not go on forever. The steps must be precise, which means each one says exactly what to do and leaves no room for guessing. The algorithm takes an input, which is the data it starts with, and it produces an output, which is the answer or result it was built to find. When you follow a recipe to bake bread, the flour and water and yeast are the input, the kneading and rising and baking are the steps, and the loaf of bread is the output.

Notice that an algorithm is not the same thing as a program. A program is an algorithm written in a specific language that a specific computer can run. The algorithm is the idea; the program is one expression of that idea. The same algorithm for finding the largest value in a list can be written as a program in dozens of languages, and it can also be described in plain English or carried out by hand with a pencil. In this section we will describe algorithms in plain language, because the ideas matter far more than the spelling of any one programming language.

Not every list of steps deserves to be called an algorithm. Computer scientists usually ask for three properties, and a good way to remember them is with the three words finite, definite, and effective. An algorithm is finite if it is guaranteed to stop after some number of steps, no matter what input it is given. A set of instructions that says keep going until you feel like stopping is not finite, because it might never end. A set of instructions that says repeat this step one hundred times and then stop is finite, because we know it will end after one hundred repetitions.

An algorithm is definite if every step is clear and unambiguous. If a step says add a little salt, a cook might add a pinch or a spoonful, and two cooks following the same recipe would get different results. A definite step would say add exactly one gram of salt. In an algorithm, each step must be so precise that two different people, or two different machines, following the same instructions on the same input would take exactly the same actions and arrive at exactly the same output. The computer cannot ask you what you meant; it can only do what the step says.

An algorithm is effective if each step is simple enough to actually be carried out. A step that says find the shortest path through this maze is not effective on its own, because it hides a whole problem inside one instruction. A step that says look at the next value in the list and compare it to the current largest value is effective, because it asks for one small, doable action. Every step must be something the machine can do directly, or something that can be broken down into smaller steps that the machine can do directly. The effective property is what connects the abstract idea of an algorithm to the physical reality of the machine that will run it.

How should we write down an algorithm? Computer scientists often use something called pseudocode, which is a careful, structured way of writing instructions in plain language. Pseudocode is not a real programming language, and no computer can run it directly, but it is precise enough that a programmer could turn it into a real program with little trouble. In this book we will use a style that is closer to ordinary prose, but we will try to keep the same precision. When we describe an algorithm, we will say exactly what values are kept track of, exactly what is done at each step, and exactly when the algorithm stops.

Before we can describe any interesting algorithm, we need the idea of a variable. A variable is a named place where a value can be stored and later changed. You can think of a variable as a labeled box. The label is the name of the variable, and the contents of the box are its current value. We might have a variable called count that holds the value zero at the start, and later holds the value five after we have counted five things. The value inside the box changes over time, but the label on the box stays the same. When an algorithm says set count to zero, it means put the value zero into the box labeled count, replacing whatever was there before.

The collection of all the variables and their current values at any moment is called the state of the algorithm. State is one of the most important ideas in all of computing. An algorithm moves forward by changing its state one step at a time. At the start, the state consists of the input and perhaps a few variables with starting values. Each step reads some of the state and changes some of the state. When the algorithm stops, the output is read from the final state. If you want to understand what an algorithm is doing at any moment, you look at its state, which means you look at the values in all of its variables. A computer is, at bottom, a machine for holding a state in its memory and changing that state according to the steps of a program.

With variables and state in hand, we can talk about the three basic ways that steps are combined. These three ways are called sequence, selection, and repetition, and remarkably, every algorithm that has ever been written can be built from just these three building blocks. Sequence means doing one step after another in order. First do this, then do that, then do the next thing. Most of the steps in a recipe are sequence: crack the eggs, then beat them, then pour them into the pan. The order matters, and the algorithm simply moves from one step to the next.

Selection means choosing between different steps depending on some condition. If the egg is cracked, throw it away; otherwise, put it in the bowl. Selection lets an algorithm respond to its input and its state. Without selection, an algorithm would do exactly the same thing every time regardless of what it was given, which would make it nearly useless. With selection, the algorithm can look at a value and decide what to do next. The condition is a question with a yes or no answer, such as is this value larger than that value, or is the list empty, and the algorithm takes one path if the answer is yes and another path if the answer is no.

Repetition means doing a step, or a group of steps, over and over again until some condition is met. Stir the soup until it boils. Walk forward until you reach the wall. Repetition is sometimes called looping, and the group of steps that is repeated is called the body of the loop. Repetition is what gives algorithms their power, because it lets a short list of instructions do a very large amount of work. An algorithm with only ten steps written down might carry out millions of actions when run, because some of those steps are inside a loop that repeats many times. Repetition also brings the greatest danger, because a loop whose condition is never met will run forever, and the algorithm will fail to be finite.

Let us now look at our first real algorithm, which is called linear search. The problem is simple: we have a list of values and a target value, and we want to know whether the target is in the list, and if so, where. Linear search solves this problem in the most direct way imaginable. Start at the beginning of the list. Look at the first value. If it is the target, stop and report its position. If it is not, move to the next value and look again. Keep going until either you find the target or you reach the end of the list. If you reach the end without finding the target, report that the target is not in the list.

Let us describe linear search a little more carefully, using a variable and the three building blocks. We keep one variable called position, which starts at one and points to the value we are currently examining. The algorithm is a loop: while position is not past the end of the list, look at the value at that position. Inside the loop there is a selection: if that value is equal to the target, stop and output the position. Otherwise, add one to position and continue the loop. After the loop ends because position has gone past the end, output a message saying the target was not found. You can see the sequence of steps, the selection that checks for a match, and the repetition that walks through the list.

Linear search is finite, because position increases by one on every pass and the list has a finite length, so the loop must eventually end. It is definite, because every step says exactly what to compare and exactly what to do. It is effective, because comparing two values and adding one to a number are things a machine can do directly. Linear search is also correct, which means it always gives the right answer. If the target is in the list, the algorithm will reach it, because it examines every position in order and never skips one. If the target is not in the list, the algorithm will examine every value, find no match, and correctly report that the target is absent.

The weakness of linear search is that it can be slow. If the list has one million values and the target is at the very end, or not in the list at all, the algorithm must look at all one million values. We say that linear search takes time proportional to the length of the list, and if we call that length n, computer scientists write this as big O of n. We will study this way of measuring algorithms much more carefully in a later section. For now, the point is simply that linear search does an amount of work that grows in step with the size of its input.

Binary search works the way you might look up a word in a printed dictionary. You do not start at the first page and read every entry; instead, you open the book somewhere in the middle and look at the words there. If the word you want comes earlier in the alphabet, you know it must be in the first half of the book, so you ignore the second half entirely. If the word comes later, you ignore the first half. Then you repeat the same trick on the half that remains, opening to its middle and throwing away half of it again. Each look cuts the remaining pages in half, so you home in on the word very quickly.

To describe binary search precisely, we keep two variables called low and high, which mark the range of positions where the target might still be. At the start, low is one and high is the length of the list, because the target could be anywhere. The algorithm is a loop that continues while low is not greater than high. Inside the loop, we compute the middle position, which is low plus high divided by two, rounded down. We look at the value at the middle position. If it equals the target, we stop and output the middle position. If the middle value is less than the target, the target must be to the right, so we set low to middle plus one. If the middle value is greater than the target, the target must be to the left, so we set high to middle minus one. If the loop ends with low greater than high, the range has become empty and the target is not in the list.

The power of binary search comes from the fact that every pass through the loop cuts the range of possible positions in half. If the list has one thousand values, the first look leaves at most five hundred, the second leaves at most two hundred fifty, and so on. After about ten looks, only one position remains. For a list of one million values, about twenty looks are enough, and for a list of one billion values, about thirty. The number of looks is roughly the number of times you can halve n before reaching one, and that number is called the logarithm of n. Computer scientists say that binary search runs in big O of log n time, which grows far more slowly than big O of n.

Binary search has a hidden cost, which is that the list must already be sorted. Keeping a list sorted takes work, and this leads us naturally to the next family of algorithms, which are the sorting algorithms. The sorting problem is to take a list of values in any order and rearrange them so they go from smallest to largest. Sorting is one of the most studied problems in all of computer science, partly because it is so useful and partly because it has so many different solutions that illustrate so many different ideas.

Our first sorting algorithm is called bubble sort, and it is probably the simplest sorting algorithm to understand, though it is far from the fastest. Bubble sort works by repeatedly walking through the list, comparing each pair of neighboring values, and swapping them if they are in the wrong order. After one full walk through the list, the largest value is guaranteed to have moved to the last position, because every time it was compared to its neighbor it was larger and so it was swapped forward. We say the largest value has bubbled up to the end. Then we walk through the list again, ignoring the last position, and the second largest value bubbles up to the second to last position. We keep walking until a full pass makes no swaps at all, which tells us the list is sorted.

Let us describe bubble sort with variables and loops. We keep a variable called swapped that records whether any swap happened during the current pass. The outer loop repeats while swapped is true. At the start of each pass, we set swapped to false. The inner loop runs a variable called position from one up to one less than the length of the unsorted part of the list. At each position, we compare the value there with the value at the next position. If the first is greater than the second, we swap them and set swapped to true. When the inner loop finishes, if swapped is still false, no pair was out of order, and the outer loop stops. Bubble sort is finite because every pass either sorts at least one more value into its final place or finds the list already sorted.

Bubble sort does a lot of comparing. In the worst case, when the list starts out in exactly reverse order, it must make about n passes, and each pass makes about n comparisons, so the total work is about n times n, which computer scientists write as big O of n squared. For a list of ten values this is fine, but for a list of one million values it means roughly one trillion comparisons, which is far too slow for practical use. Bubble sort is mainly taught because it is easy to understand, not because anyone uses it for serious work.

Our second sorting algorithm is called insertion sort, and it is the way most people naturally sort a hand of playing cards. You hold the cards you have already sorted in one hand, and you pick up the next card with the other. You find the place where the new card belongs among the sorted cards, slide the larger cards over to make room, and insert the new card into its spot. After you have picked up every card, your whole hand is sorted. The key idea is that the sorted part of the list grows by one value at a time, and each new value is inserted into the correct position within the already sorted part.

In terms of variables and loops, insertion sort keeps a boundary between the sorted part at the front of the list and the unsorted part at the back. At the start, the sorted part contains only the first value, since a list of one value is trivially sorted. The outer loop takes each value from the unsorted part in turn. The inner loop moves backward through the sorted part, shifting each value that is larger than the new value one position to the right, until it finds a value that is smaller or reaches the front of the list. Then the new value is placed in the gap that was opened. The boundary moves forward by one, and the outer loop continues until the unsorted part is empty.

Insertion sort also takes big O of n squared time in the worst case, because each new value might need to be shifted past every value already sorted. But insertion sort has a nice property that bubble sort lacks. If the list is already nearly sorted, insertion sort runs very quickly, because each new value needs to be shifted only a short distance or not at all. In that case the time is close to big O of n. For this reason insertion sort is often used in practice for small lists or for lists that are known to be almost in order already.

Our third simple sorting algorithm is called selection sort, and it takes yet another approach to the same problem. Selection sort repeatedly finds the smallest value in the unsorted part of the list and moves it to the end of the sorted part. On the first pass, it scans the whole list to find the smallest value and swaps it into the first position. On the second pass, it scans the remaining values to find the smallest among them and swaps it into the second position. It continues in this way until every position has been filled with the right value. Where insertion sort takes the next value and finds its place, selection sort takes the next place and finds its value.

Selection sort keeps a variable called boundary that marks the first position of the unsorted part, and a variable called smallest that remembers the position of the smallest value seen so far during a scan. The outer loop runs boundary from one up to the length of the list. Inside, we set smallest to boundary and then run an inner loop over every position from boundary plus one to the end, updating smallest whenever we find a value less than the value at smallest. After the inner loop, we swap the value at boundary with the value at smallest. Selection sort always takes big O of n squared time, even on a sorted list, because it must scan the entire unsorted part on every pass to be sure it has found the smallest value.

All three of the simple sorting algorithms take big O of n squared time, and for a long while it might seem that this is the best anyone can do. But there is a much faster sorting algorithm called merge sort, and understanding it requires a new way of thinking about problems. Merge sort is built on the idea of dividing a problem into smaller pieces, solving each piece, and then combining the solutions. This idea is called divide and conquer, and it appears again and again throughout computer science.

Merge sort begins with a simple observation: if you have two lists that are each already sorted, you can combine them into one sorted list very quickly. You look at the first value of each list, take the smaller of the two, and put it at the end of the output. Then you look at the new first values and do the same thing again. You keep taking the smaller front value until one list is empty, and then you copy the rest of the other list to the output. This combining step is called merging, and it takes time proportional to the total number of values, which is big O of n.

Now here is the clever part. To sort a list using merge sort, split it into two halves. Sort each half, and then merge the two sorted halves together. But how do we sort each half? We use merge sort again. We split each half into two quarters, sort each quarter, and merge them. And how do we sort each quarter? We split it again. We keep splitting until we reach lists of just one value, and a list of one value is already sorted, so there is nothing to do. Then we merge the single values into sorted pairs, merge the pairs into sorted groups of four, and so on, until the whole list is merged back together in sorted order.

Merge sort takes big O of n log n time, which is a dramatic improvement over big O of n squared. The reason is that the list is split into halves about log n times, and at each level of splitting, the total work of merging all the pieces together is about n. For a list of one million values, n squared is about one trillion, while n log n is about twenty million. The difference between those two numbers is the difference between an algorithm that finishes in a fraction of a second and one that takes many minutes. This is why the choice of algorithm matters so much more than the speed of the machine.

The description of merge sort has a strange feature that the earlier algorithms did not have. The algorithm uses itself as one of its own steps. To sort a list, it sorts two smaller lists, and to sort those it sorts even smaller lists. An algorithm that calls itself in this way is called recursive, and recursion is one of the deepest ideas in computer science. It can feel like a trick or even like cheating when you first meet it, because it seems to define something in terms of itself. The key to seeing why it works is that each call to the algorithm is on a strictly smaller input, so the chain of calls must eventually reach an input so small that it can be handled directly.

The input that can be handled directly, without any further recursive call, is called the base case. In merge sort, the base case is a list of one value, which is already sorted. Every recursive algorithm must have at least one base case, and every recursive call must move closer to a base case. If either of these conditions fails, the algorithm will call itself forever, never reaching an answer, and it will fail to be finite. Forgetting the base case is the most common mistake that beginners make when they first write recursive programs, and the result is a program that runs until the machine runs out of memory.

Let us look at a simpler example of recursion than merge sort. Suppose we want to compute the factorial of a number n, which is the product of all whole numbers from one up to n. The factorial of five is one times two times three times four times five, which is one hundred twenty. A recursive description says: the factorial of one is one, and that is the base case. For any larger n, the factorial of n is n times the factorial of n minus one. To compute the factorial of five, we compute the factorial of four and multiply by five. To compute the factorial of four, we compute the factorial of three and multiply by four. The chain continues down to the factorial of one, which we know directly, and then the multiplications happen on the way back up.

The same problem can be solved without recursion, using an ordinary loop. We keep a variable called result that starts at one, and a variable called counter that runs from one up to n. At each pass through the loop, we multiply result by counter. When the loop ends, result holds the factorial of n. This loop based approach is called iteration, and it uses repetition instead of recursion. Both approaches compute the same function and give the same output for the same input. The recursive version is often shorter and closer to the mathematical definition, while the iterative version is often faster on a real machine and uses less memory.

To understand why recursion uses memory, we need to look at how a computer keeps track of a recursive algorithm. When an algorithm calls itself, the computer must remember where it was in the outer call so that it can return there after the inner call finishes. It must also remember the values of all the variables in the outer call, because the inner call will have its own variables with the same names but different values. The computer stores all of this information in a region of memory called the call stack.

A stack is a structure where items are added to the top and removed from the top, like a stack of plates. When a function is called, a new block of information called a frame is pushed onto the top of the call stack. The frame holds the variables for that call and a note of where to return when the call finishes. When the function finishes, its frame is popped off the top, and the computer continues from the return point stored in the frame below. For recursive calls, the stack grows one frame deeper with each call. Computing the factorial of five pushes five frames, one for each value from five down to one, and then pops them off one at a time as the multiplications complete.

The call stack has a limited size, and if recursion goes too deep, the stack runs out of room. This failure is called a stack overflow, and it is the error you get when you forget a base case or when a problem is simply too large for the recursive approach. The iterative factorial algorithm, by contrast, uses only two variables no matter how large n is, so it never faces this problem. This is the main practical reason that programmers sometimes convert recursive algorithms into iterative ones, even though the recursive version may be easier to read and to prove correct.

That brings us to the question of correctness. How do we know that an algorithm actually solves the problem it claims to solve? For a small input we can simply run the algorithm by hand and check the output, but no amount of testing on small inputs can prove that an algorithm works on every possible input. For that we need an argument, which is a reasoned explanation of why the algorithm must produce the right output whatever input it is given. Computer scientists have developed a powerful tool for building such arguments, and it is called the invariant.

An invariant is a statement about the state of an algorithm that is true at a particular point every time the algorithm reaches that point. The most useful kind is the loop invariant, which is a statement that is true before the loop starts, remains true after every pass through the loop body, and together with the condition that ends the loop implies that the algorithm has produced the correct output. If we can show those three things, we have proved that the loop does its job. The invariant acts like a thread that ties together every pass of the loop and guarantees that progress is being made toward the goal.

Consider the loop in insertion sort. A good invariant is this: at the start of each pass, the values in the sorted part of the list are the same values that were originally in those positions, but arranged in sorted order. Before the loop starts, the sorted part contains only one value, and a single value is trivially in sorted order, so the invariant holds. Each pass takes the next value and inserts it into the right place among the sorted values, so after the pass the sorted part is one longer and still in order, and the invariant still holds. When the loop ends, the sorted part is the whole list, so the invariant tells us the whole list is sorted. That is a complete argument for the correctness of insertion sort.

Correctness arguments for recursive algorithms use a closely related idea called induction. We show that the algorithm is correct on the base case, and we show that if it is correct on all smaller inputs, then it is correct on a larger input. Together, those two facts prove it is correct on every input. For merge sort, the base case is a list of one value, which is already sorted. If merge sort correctly sorts each half, and merging two sorted lists produces a sorted list, then merge sort correctly sorts the whole list. The structure of the proof mirrors the structure of the algorithm, which is one reason recursive algorithms are often easier to reason about than iterative ones.

We have now seen four different algorithms that all solve the sorting problem, and this leads to an important lesson. A single problem can have many different algorithms, and they can differ enormously in speed, in memory use, in simplicity, and in how well they handle special cases. Bubble sort, insertion sort, and selection sort all produce the same sorted output, but they reach it by different paths and with different amounts of work. Merge sort reaches the same output far faster, but it needs extra memory to hold the lists it merges and it needs the machinery of recursion. There are many more sorting algorithms beyond these four, each with its own strengths and weaknesses.

The same lesson applies to searching, where linear search and binary search solve the same problem. Linear search works on any list but is slow, while binary search is fast but demands a sorted list. If you need to search a list only once, sorting it first would be a waste of effort, and linear search is the better choice. If you need to search the same list thousands of times, sorting it once and then using binary search every time will pay off handsomely. Choosing the right algorithm means thinking about the whole problem, including how the input arrives, how often the algorithm will be run, and how much time and memory are available.

Before moving on, it is worth stepping back to see how much we have covered. An algorithm is a finite, definite, effective list of steps that transforms input into output. Algorithms are built from variables that hold state, and from the three building blocks of sequence, selection, and repetition. We can describe them in careful prose, and we can reason about their correctness using invariants and induction. Some algorithms call themselves, and the computer manages those calls with a call stack that grows and shrinks as the recursion goes deeper and returns. Every one of these ideas will reappear throughout the rest of this book, because they are the foundation on which everything else in computer science is built.


In the previous sections we looked at how a computer represents information as bits and how an algorithm turns an input into an output through a sequence of steps. In this section we turn to a question that sits between those two ideas. How should a program arrange its data in memory so that the algorithm can do its work quickly? The answer to that question is the subject of data structures, and it is one of the most practical topics in all of computer science.

A data structure is a particular way of organizing a collection of values inside a computer so that certain operations on that collection are fast and convenient. Every data structure makes a promise about what it can do well and an honest admission about what it does poorly. A good programmer learns to match the structure to the problem. Much of the skill in writing efficient programs comes from choosing the right structure before writing the first line of the algorithm.

Why does the arrangement of data in memory matter so much to a program? Consider a simple problem: you have a list of one million names and you want to know whether a particular name is in the list. If the names are stored in no particular order, the only thing you can do is look at each one in turn. That takes about one million steps in the worst case. If the names are stored in sorted order, you can use a much cleverer algorithm that takes about twenty steps. If the names are stored in a hash table, the lookup takes roughly one step on average. The input is the same, the output is the same, and yet the amount of work differs by a factor of a million. That enormous difference in work comes entirely from the choice of structure.

This is the central lesson of the whole section: an algorithm and a data structure are two halves of one idea. The algorithm describes the steps, and the data structure describes the shape of the memory those steps operate on. You cannot judge one half without looking at the other half. When we say that an algorithm takes big O of n time or big O of log n time, we are always assuming some particular structure underneath it.

Let us begin with the simplest and most fundamental structure of all, the array. An array is a block of contiguous memory holding a fixed number of elements of the same size, one after another with no gaps. Suppose each element occupies four bytes and the array begins at some address in memory. Then the element at position zero lives at that address, and the element at position one lives four bytes later. The element at position two lives eight bytes later, and so on down the block. The computer can compute the address of any element with a single multiplication and a single addition.

That simple arithmetic is the entire secret of the array structure. The address of the element at position i is just the starting address plus i times the element size. The computer can therefore jump directly to any element in one step. We say that array access is constant time, or big O of one, and this holds no matter whether the array has ten elements or ten million. This property is called random access, and it is the array's greatest strength.

Arrays have a second strength that is less obvious but just as important in practice. Because the elements sit next to each other in memory, the hardware that moves data between main memory and the processor can fetch many neighboring elements at once. Modern computers have small, very fast memories called caches, and when a program reads one element of an array the cache usually pulls in its neighbors for free. A program that walks through an array from start to finish therefore runs much faster than the raw count of steps would suggest. This is a case where the physical layout of the machine rewards a particular structure.

The weaknesses of the array are the mirror image of its strengths. Because the block of memory is fixed in size, you cannot simply add an element past the end if the space beyond the array is occupied by something else. To grow an array you must allocate a larger block somewhere else, copy every element across, and release the old block. That copy takes big O of n steps, one for each element moved. Inserting an element in the middle of an array is also expensive, because every element after the insertion point must shift one position to the right. Deleting an element in the middle is just as bad, because every element after it must shift one position to the left.

Programmers often use a trick called a dynamic array to soften the cost of growth. A dynamic array keeps a little extra space at the end, and when that space runs out it allocates a new block that is twice as large. Doubling means that the expensive copy of the whole block happens only rarely. If you spread the cost of the copies across all the insertions, each insertion costs only a constant amount on average. This kind of averaged accounting is called amortized analysis, and it is a recurring idea in the study of data structures.

The array is the right choice when you know roughly how many elements you have and need to jump to an arbitrary position quickly. It also works well when you mostly add and remove elements at the end. It is the wrong choice when you constantly insert and delete elements in the middle, or when the collection grows and shrinks unpredictably and you cannot afford the copies. For those situations we need a structure that is not tied to a single contiguous block.

The linked list is that structure, and it takes a very different approach. Instead of placing elements side by side, a linked list stores each element in its own small container called a node. Each node holds a value and a reference, often called a pointer, to the next node in the list. The nodes can be scattered anywhere in memory, and the list is held together only by the chain of references. The program keeps a reference to the first node, called the head, and from the head it can follow the chain to reach every other node in turn.

Inserting a new element into a linked list is beautifully cheap once you are standing at the right place. To insert after some node, you create the new node and point it at whatever the current node was pointing to. Then you point the current node at the new one. Two reference updates are all it takes, and the insertion is done. No elements need to shift, and no memory needs to be copied. Deleting a node is similar: you make the previous node point past the node you want to remove, and the removed node simply falls out of the chain.

The price of this flexibility is the loss of random access. In a linked list there is no arithmetic that takes you directly to the element at position i, because the nodes are not laid out at predictable addresses. To reach the tenth node you must start at the head and follow nine references, one step at a time. Access by position is therefore big O of n, and a program that needs to look up elements by position should not use a linked list. The list also pays a space cost: every node carries a reference alongside its value, and for small values the reference can take as much memory as the data itself.

The structure we have just described is a singly linked list, because each node knows only about the node after it. A doubly linked list gives each node two references, one to the next node and one to the previous node. This costs a little more space per node, but it buys the ability to walk the list backward. More importantly, it lets you delete a node when you hold a reference only to that node and not to its predecessor. In a singly linked list, deleting a node requires knowing the node before it, which may mean walking the whole chain from the head. In a doubly linked list, the node itself tells you where its neighbors are, so the deletion takes a constant number of steps.

With arrays and lists in hand, we can describe two of the most widely used structures in all of programming, the stack and the queue. Both are collections where you add and remove elements, and both restrict where the adding and removing can happen. That restriction on where elements enter and leave is the whole point of the design. By limiting what the structure allows, we make the allowed operations extremely fast and we make the behavior of the structure easy to reason about.

A stack is a collection where elements are added and removed from the same end, which we call the top. Adding an element is called pushing, and removing an element is called popping. The element that comes out when you pop is always the one that was pushed most recently. We say that a stack is last in, first out. Think of a stack of clean plates sitting on a shelf in a kitchen. You put a clean plate on top, and when you need a plate you take the top one, so the last plate added is the first one taken.

A stack can be implemented with a dynamic array by always pushing and popping at the end, where the array is efficient. It can also be implemented with a singly linked list by always pushing and popping at the head. Either way, both the push operation and the pop operation take constant time. Stacks appear everywhere in computing, often in places where you would not expect them. When a program calls a function, the computer pushes a record of where to return onto a stack, and when the function finishes the computer pops that record and resumes. This is why the memory region that tracks function calls is called the call stack, and why an infinitely recursive function eventually causes a stack overflow.

Stacks are also the natural structure for any problem that involves nesting or reversal. To check whether parentheses in an expression are balanced, you push each opening parenthesis and pop when you see a closing one. If the stack is empty when you need to pop, or nonempty at the end, the expression is unbalanced. To undo a sequence of edits in a text editor, you push each edit as it happens and pop to undo the most recent one. Any time the most recent thing is the thing you need next, reach for a stack.

A queue is the opposite discipline, and it enforces the opposite order. Elements are added at one end, called the back or tail, and removed from the other end, called the front or head. The element that comes out is always the one that has been waiting longest. We say that a queue is first in, first out, and the obvious picture is a line of people waiting at a counter. The person who arrived first is served first, and newcomers join at the back.

Implementing a queue with a plain array is awkward, because removing from the front would require shifting every element forward. The usual solution is a circular array, where the front and back positions wrap around to the beginning of the block when they reach the end. With a circular array, both adding and removing are constant time. A doubly linked list also works nicely, because it supports constant time operations at both ends. Queues are used wherever fairness or the order of arrival matters to the program. Examples include tasks waiting for a processor, messages waiting to be delivered, and nodes waiting to be visited in a graph search.

We now come to one of the most important structures in practice, the hash table. Recall the problem of checking whether a name is in a collection of one million names. An array requires a linear scan, and a sorted array allows a logarithmic search, which we will explore later. A hash table, however, allows a lookup that takes a constant number of steps on average. The idea behind it is one of the cleverest tricks in computer science.

The key ingredient of the whole structure is a hash function. A hash function takes a value, such as a name, and produces a number, usually a fairly large one, in a way that looks random but is completely deterministic. The same input always produces the same output, and different inputs usually produce different outputs. A simple hash function for a string might multiply a running total by some constant and add the code of each character in turn. At each step it takes the remainder by a large number to keep the total from growing too big.

A hash table is simply an array of slots, often called buckets, together with a hash function. To store a value, you apply the hash function to it, take the result modulo the number of slots, and place the value in the slot with that index. To look a value up, you compute the same index and look in that slot. If the hash function spreads values evenly across the slots, then each slot holds only a few values. Both insertion and lookup then take a constant number of steps on average. The array gives us constant time access by index, and the hash function turns an arbitrary value into an index.

Of course, two different values can hash to the same slot, and this event is called a collision. Collisions are unavoidable whenever there are more possible values than slots, and a hash table must have a plan for them. One plan is called chaining: each slot holds a small linked list of all the values that hashed there, and a lookup walks that list. Another plan is called open addressing: if a slot is already occupied, the table probes forward to the next slot, and then the next, until it finds an empty one. Both approaches work well as long as the table does not become too full.

The fullness of a hash table is measured by its load factor, which is the number of stored values divided by the number of slots. When the load factor climbs too high, the chains grow long or the probing sequences grow long, and the constant time guarantee evaporates. The standard remedy is the same trick we used for dynamic arrays. When the load factor crosses a threshold, allocate a larger array, typically twice as large, and reinsert every value with the new modulus. This rehashing costs big O of n but happens rarely, so the amortized cost per operation stays constant.

The hash table pays for its speed with two limitations. First, it offers no notion of order: you cannot ask a hash table for the smallest value, or for all values between two bounds, without examining every slot. Second, its performance is excellent on average but not guaranteed in every case, since an unlucky pattern of collisions can slow it down. When order matters or when a strict worst case guarantee matters, we need a different structure, and that brings us to trees.

A tree is a structure made of nodes connected by references, like a linked list, but where each node can point to more than one other node. One node is designated as the root, and every other node has exactly one parent and zero or more children. Nodes with no children at all are called the leaves of the tree. The number of steps from the root to a given node is that node's depth, and the depth of the deepest node is the height of the tree. The structure is called a tree because, drawn with the root at the top, it branches out like an upside down tree.

A binary tree is a tree in which each node has at most two children, conventionally called the left child and the right child. Each node holds a value together with two references, either of which may be empty. A binary tree with n nodes can have many different shapes. In the worst case it is a long chain where every node has only one child, which is really just a linked list and has height n. In the best case it is perfectly balanced, with every level full except possibly the last, and its height is about log base two of n.

That height difference is the reason trees are interesting to a programmer. Suppose an operation on a tree takes a number of steps proportional to the height. Then a balanced tree with a million nodes requires only about twenty steps, while a degenerate chain requires a million. Nearly everything we do with trees comes down to keeping the height small.

A binary search tree is a binary tree with one additional rule. For every node, every value in its left subtree is smaller than the node's value, and every value in its right subtree is larger. This ordering rule makes searching the structure for a value simple. To find a value, start at the root and compare. If the value you want is smaller, go left; if it is larger, go right; if it is equal, you have found it. Each comparison eliminates an entire subtree, so a search takes a number of steps proportional to the height.

Insertion into a binary search tree follows the same path as a search. Search for the value, and when you reach an empty reference, place the new node there. The ordering rule is preserved automatically, because you arrived at that spot by following comparisons. Deletion is a little trickier, especially when the node to be removed has two children, but it can also be done in time proportional to the height. The binary search tree therefore gives us search, insertion, and deletion all in big O of h time, where h is the height.

Unlike a hash table, a binary search tree keeps its values in order. Walking the tree in a pattern called in order traversal, which visits the left subtree, then the node, then the right subtree, produces every value in sorted order. Finding the minimum means following left references until you cannot, and finding the maximum means following right references. Finding all values between two bounds, or finding the smallest value greater than a given one, are natural operations on a search tree and awkward ones on a hash table.

The trouble is that a plain binary search tree does nothing to control its own height. If you insert values in sorted order, each new value is larger than everything already in the tree. It therefore goes to the right of the rightmost node, and the tree becomes a chain. Search then degrades to big O of n, no better than scanning a list. The solution is a family of structures called balanced search trees, which perform small rearrangements after each insertion or deletion to keep the height close to log n.

An AVL tree, named after its two inventors, is a binary search tree that stores in each node the difference between the heights of its left and right subtrees. The tree insists that this difference never exceed one at any node. After an insertion or deletion, the tree walks back up toward the root checking this condition. Where the condition is violated, the tree performs an operation called a rotation. A rotation is a local rearrangement of a node and one of its children that preserves the ordering rule but shifts height from one side to the other. A single rotation or a pair of rotations is always enough to restore balance. Because the walk back up is along one path, the whole operation stays within big O of log n.

A red black tree takes a slightly looser approach to the same problem. Each node is colored either red or black, and the tree enforces a few rules about colors. The root is black, no red node has a red child, and every path from a node down to a leaf passes through the same number of black nodes. These rules do not force perfect balance, but they guarantee that no path from the root is more than twice as long as any other. That guarantee is enough to keep the height within a small constant factor of log n. Insertion and deletion fix any broken rules with a handful of recolorings and rotations.

The difference between the two is a matter of trade-offs. AVL trees are more strictly balanced, so searches are slightly faster, but they may perform more rotations during insertion and deletion. Red black trees allow a little more imbalance in exchange for cheaper updates. Many standard libraries use red black trees for their ordered map and ordered set structures. The typical workload mixes reads and writes, and the red black tree handles that mix well. For a beginner, the important point is not the exact rules of either tree. The important point is that a balanced tree gives logarithmic time for search, insertion, deletion, and ordered traversal all at once.

A different kind of tree answers a different kind of question. Suppose you do not need to find arbitrary values but only need to find the smallest value quickly, remove it, and insert new values as they arrive. This is exactly the set of operations that a priority queue requires. A priority queue is like a queue, except that the element removed next is not the one that waited longest. Instead it is the one with the highest priority, which we can represent as the smallest or largest key. The structure that implements a priority queue efficiently is the heap.

A binary heap is a binary tree that satisfies two conditions. First, it is complete, meaning every level is full except the last, which is filled from the left. Second, it satisfies a rule called the heap property, which depends on the kind of heap. In a min heap, every node's value is less than or equal to the values of its children. In a max heap, every node's value is greater than or equal to the values of its children. The heap property says nothing about the relationship between left and right siblings, which makes it much weaker than the search tree rule and much cheaper to maintain.

The completeness condition has a wonderful consequence for how the heap is stored. A complete binary tree can be stored in a plain array without any references at all. If the root is at index zero, the children of the node at index i are at indices two i plus one and two i plus two. The parent of the node at index i is at index i minus one divided by two, with the remainder discarded. The tree structure is implicit in the arithmetic, so the heap enjoys the compact storage and cache friendliness of an array while behaving like a tree.

Finding the minimum of a min heap is trivial, since it is always at the root. Removing the minimum from the heap takes a few more steps than finding it. Move the last element of the array to the root, then repeatedly swap it with its smaller child until the heap property holds again. This process, often called sifting down, takes at most as many steps as the height of the tree, which is about log n because a complete tree is always balanced. Inserting a new value is the reverse: place it at the end of the array and repeatedly swap it with its parent while it is smaller than the parent. This is called sifting up and also takes big O of log n.

We now turn to a structure that is less a single thing and more a whole family: the graph. A graph is a collection of nodes, often called vertices, together with a collection of edges, each of which connects two nodes. A tree is a special case of a graph with no cycles and a single root. A general graph can have cycles, can have several disconnected pieces, and need not have any root at all. Edges can be undirected, meaning they connect two nodes symmetrically, or directed, meaning they point from one node to another. Edges may also carry weights, such as the distance between two cities or the cost of a connection.

There are two standard ways to store a graph in memory, and the choice between them is a classic example of the time versus space trade-off. The first representation of a graph in memory is called the adjacency matrix. Number the nodes from zero to n minus one, and create an n by n array of values. The entry at row i and column j is one if there is an edge from node i to node j, and zero otherwise. For a weighted graph, the entry holds the weight of the edge instead. Checking whether two particular nodes are connected is a single array access, which is constant time.

The cost of an adjacency matrix is the amount of space it uses. It uses n squared entries no matter how many edges the graph actually has. For a graph with ten thousand nodes, that is one hundred million entries, even if the graph has only twenty thousand edges. Most real graphs are sparse, meaning the number of edges is much smaller than n squared, and for sparse graphs the matrix wastes nearly all of its space on zeros. Listing the neighbors of a node also takes big O of n time, because you must scan an entire row.

The second representation of a graph in memory is called the adjacency list. For each node in the graph, store a list of the nodes it is connected to. This is usually an array of n lists, where the list at index i contains the neighbors of node i. The total space used is proportional to the number of nodes plus the number of edges, which for a sparse graph is enormously smaller than n squared. Listing the neighbors of a node is now proportional to the number of neighbors, which is exactly what graph algorithms usually need. The price is that checking whether a specific edge exists requires scanning the neighbor list of one endpoint. A hash set per node can restore constant time for that check at the cost of a little more memory.

The adjacency list is the default choice for most graph work. The adjacency matrix is reserved for dense graphs or for problems where the matrix form enables a particular algorithm. Here we see once again that the same information can be laid out in two different ways. The right layout depends on which questions the program will ask most often.

The final structure we will examine is the trie, which is a tree specialized for storing strings. The name comes from the middle of the word retrieval, though it is usually pronounced like the word try. In a trie, each node represents a prefix, and each edge is labeled with a single character. The root of the trie represents the empty string with no characters. To store the word cat, you follow or create an edge labeled c from the root, then an edge labeled a from that node. Then you follow an edge labeled t and mark the final node as the end of a word.

The power of the trie comes from sharing common prefixes between words. The words cat, car, and cart all share the prefix ca, so they share the same path from the root for those two characters and only branch apart afterward. Looking up a word takes a number of steps equal to the length of the word, regardless of how many words are stored. This makes a trie an excellent structure for a dictionary. More importantly, a trie can answer questions that a hash table cannot. All words beginning with a given prefix are exactly the words below the node for that prefix, so autocomplete becomes a simple traversal of a subtree.

The cost of a trie, as with so many fast structures, is space. Each node must store references for every possible next character, and if the alphabet has twenty six letters, a naive node holds twenty six references, most of them empty. Various refinements reduce this cost, such as storing the children in a small hash table or a sorted list. Another refinement compresses chains of nodes with a single child into one node with a multi character label. The trie is a reminder that even a structure with wonderful time behavior must be weighed against its demands on memory.

Having toured the main structures, we can step back and consider the trade-offs that govern the choice among them. Three concerns pull against one another in nearly every decision: time, space, and simplicity. Time is how many steps the operations of the structure take. Space is how much memory the structure consumes beyond the raw data. Simplicity is how easy the structure is to implement correctly, to understand, and to debug. No structure wins on all three, and the art lies in deciding which to sacrifice.

Consider the problem of storing a set of values and checking membership. An unsorted array is simplest and uses no extra space, but membership takes big O of n time. A sorted array allows binary search in big O of log n time with no extra space, but insertion costs big O of n. A balanced tree gives big O of log n for everything and keeps the values ordered. However, it uses extra space for references and balance information, and it is notoriously difficult to implement without mistakes. A hash table gives constant time on average with moderate extra space, but it loses ordering and offers only average case guarantees. Each option is the best answer for some problem and the wrong answer for others.

Simplicity deserves more respect than it usually receives from beginners. A simpler structure has fewer places for bugs to hide, and a bug in a data structure can corrupt every piece of data that passes through it. For a small collection, the constant factors hidden inside big O notation matter more than the growth rate. A plain array scanned linearly will often beat a balanced tree for collections of a few dozen elements. Many experienced programmers start with the simplest structure that could possibly work and replace it with something cleverer only after measuring that the simple version is actually too slow.

Let us close with a brief guide to choosing a structure for a problem. If you need fast access by position and the collection is mostly stable in size, use an array. If you need frequent insertion and deletion at arbitrary known positions and never need access by index, use a linked list. If you need to process items in reverse order of arrival, use a stack; in order of arrival, a queue; in order of priority, a heap. If you need fast membership tests or fast lookup by key and do not care about order, use a hash table. If you need ordered operations such as finding the next larger value or walking the values in sorted order, use a balanced search tree. If you need to work with strings sharing common prefixes, use a trie. If your problem is about things and the relationships between them, use a graph, stored as an adjacency list unless the graph is dense.

These rules are starting points rather than laws, and every real program will bend them. But there is one habit worth keeping: ask which operations the program will perform most often, and then choose the structure whose strengths match those operations. That habit separates a program that merely works from a program that works well. The algorithm you write in the next section, and the machine model you study after that, will both rest on the structures described here. A computer is a device for transforming input into output through steps, and a data structure is the shape that makes those steps cheap.


When we write an algorithm, we usually want to know two things about it. We want to know that it produces the correct output for every valid input, and we want to know how much time and space it needs to do so. The previous sections of this book dealt mostly with the first question. This section deals with the second question, which is the subject known as the analysis of algorithms. The analysis of algorithms is the study of how the running time and the memory use of an algorithm grow as the size of the input grows. It gives us a language for comparing two algorithms that solve the same problem, and it gives us a way to predict how an algorithm will behave on inputs far larger than any we have actually tried.

The first thing to settle is what we mean by running time. We could simply run the algorithm on a computer and measure the number of seconds it takes, but this measurement depends on many things that have nothing to do with the algorithm itself. A faster processor will finish sooner, a different programming language will produce different machine code, and even the same program on the same machine will take slightly different times on different runs. If we want to talk about the algorithm rather than about a particular machine, we need a measure that does not change when the machine changes. The usual answer is to count steps. A step is a basic operation such as adding two numbers, comparing two values, reading one value from memory, or assigning a value to a variable. We assume that every basic operation takes some fixed amount of time, and we count how many basic operations the algorithm performs as a function of the size of its input.

Consider a simple example. Suppose we have a list of n numbers and we want to find the largest one. A natural algorithm keeps a running record of the largest value seen so far. It starts by setting that record to the first value in the list, and then it walks through the remaining values one at a time. For each value it performs one comparison with the current record, and if the new value is larger it performs one assignment to update the record. The algorithm looks at each of the n values once, so it performs about n comparisons and at most n assignments. If we are careful we might say it performs one assignment at the start, then n minus one comparisons, then somewhere between zero and n minus one updates, plus a little bookkeeping to move from one position in the list to the next. The total is some expression like three n plus two, or perhaps five n minus three, depending on exactly how we decide to count.

That last remark points to a problem. Two careful people counting the steps of the same algorithm might arrive at different expressions, because they disagree about what counts as one step. One person might count the increment of a loop variable as a separate step; another might fold it into the comparison. One machine might perform an addition in one cycle and a comparison in two cycles, while another machine does the reverse. If our measure of running time changes every time we change our mind about these small details, it is not a very useful measure. We need a way to describe running time that is insensitive to these details while still capturing what matters.

The resolution is to ignore constant factors and lower order terms. When we say that the running time of the largest value algorithm is proportional to n, we are saying that doubling the size of the input roughly doubles the number of steps, and that is true whether the exact count is three n plus two or five n minus three. The constant in front of n depends on the machine and on our counting conventions; the fact that the time grows in proportion to n depends only on the algorithm. The constant is a property of the implementation, and the growth rate is a property of the idea. Since we want to study ideas, we keep the growth rate and discard the constant.

There is a second reason to ignore constants, and it has to do with what happens when inputs become large. Suppose one algorithm takes one hundred n steps and another takes n squared steps. For small inputs the second algorithm looks better; when n is ten, the first algorithm takes one thousand steps and the second takes only one hundred. But when n is one thousand, the first takes one hundred thousand steps and the second takes one million. When n is one million, the first takes one hundred million steps and the second takes one trillion. No matter how large the constant in front of n is, there is some input size beyond which n squared is larger, and beyond that point the gap only widens. For large inputs, the growth rate dominates and the constant becomes irrelevant. Since computers are most useful precisely when inputs are large, the growth rate is what we care about most.

This way of thinking is called asymptotic analysis, because it describes the behavior of a function as its argument tends toward infinity. Computer scientists use a small family of notations to express asymptotic statements, and the most famous of these is big O notation. When we say that a function f of n is big O of g of n, we mean that for all sufficiently large n, the value of f of n is at most some constant times the value of g of n. In plain words, f grows no faster than g, once we allow ourselves to multiply g by a fixed constant and to ignore small values of n. Big O gives an upper bound on the growth of a function. If an algorithm's running time is big O of n squared, we know it does not grow faster than n squared, although it might grow more slowly.

The statement that three n plus two is big O of n is true, because for every n of size at least one, three n plus two is at most five times n. The statement that three n plus two is big O of n squared is also true, because n squared eventually exceeds any constant multiple of n. This second statement is less informative, since it tells us less about the actual growth of the function, but it is not false. When we use big O in practice we try to give the tightest bound we can, but it is worth remembering that big O by itself only promises an upper bound.

To express a lower bound, we use big Omega notation. When we say that f of n is big Omega of g of n, we mean that for all sufficiently large n, f of n is at least some positive constant times g of n. In plain words, f grows at least as fast as g. If we know that an algorithm's running time is big Omega of n, we know that it must take at least a number of steps proportional to n, and so it cannot be made to run in constant time no matter how clever the implementation. Lower bounds tell us what we cannot avoid. For the largest value problem, any algorithm must look at every value in the list at least once, because a value it never looks at might be the largest, so the running time is big Omega of n.

When a function is both big O of g and big Omega of g, we say that it is big Theta of g. Big Theta is the notation for a tight bound. It says that f grows at the same rate as g, up to constant factors, neither faster nor slower. The running time of the largest value algorithm is big Theta of n: it is at most a constant times n and at least a constant times n. In everyday speech, people often say big O when they really mean big Theta, and they say that an algorithm is big O of n squared when they mean that it takes time proportional to n squared. This loose usage is common and usually harmless, but a careful writer distinguishes the two, and in this book we will try to be careful.

With this vocabulary in place, we can survey the growth rates that appear most often in practice. The slowest growing rate is constant time, written big O of one. An algorithm with constant running time takes the same number of steps regardless of the size of its input. Looking up a value at a known position in an array is constant time, because the computer can compute the memory address directly and read the value without examining anything else. Checking whether a number is even is constant time. Pushing a value onto the top of a stack is constant time. Constant time operations are the building blocks from which everything else is assembled.

The next rate is logarithmic time, written big O of log n. The logarithm of n, in this context, is roughly the number of times you can halve n before reaching one. The logarithm of one thousand twenty four is ten, since halving ten times brings one thousand twenty four down to one. The logarithm of one million is about twenty, and the logarithm of one billion is about thirty. Logarithmic time is extraordinarily fast; an algorithm that takes logarithmic time on an input of one billion values performs only around thirty steps. Binary search is the classic example. If a list is sorted, we can find a value by comparing it with the middle element, discarding the half of the list that cannot contain it, and repeating. Each comparison halves the remaining search space, so the number of comparisons is at most the logarithm of n. The base of the logarithm does not matter in asymptotic notation, because logarithms with different bases differ only by a constant factor.

The next rate is linear time, written big O of n. A linear time algorithm does an amount of work proportional to the size of its input. Finding the largest value in an unsorted list is linear, as we saw. Summing a list of numbers is linear. Reading a file from beginning to end is linear in the length of the file. Linear time is usually the best one can hope for in any problem that requires examining the whole input, because simply looking at each value once already takes n steps. When an algorithm runs in linear time, we often say it is as fast as it could possibly be, and this is nearly true: it cannot be beaten by more than a constant factor.

Slightly above linear is the rate called n log n, written big O of n log n. This rate arises whenever an algorithm does logarithmic work for each of n values, or linear work at each of log n levels. The fastest general sorting algorithms, such as merge sort and heap sort, run in n log n time. Because log n grows so slowly, n log n is only modestly larger than n in practice; for an input of one million values, n log n is about twenty million, compared with one million for a linear algorithm. An n log n algorithm is still considered very efficient, and for many problems it is the best known growth rate.

Quadratic time, written big O of n squared, is the next common rate. A quadratic algorithm typically examines every pair of values in the input. The simplest sorting algorithms, such as bubble sort, selection sort, and insertion sort, are quadratic in the worst case, because each of the n values may need to be compared with each of the other n values. Checking whether any two values in a list are equal by comparing every pair is quadratic. Quadratic algorithms are fine for small inputs and become painful for large ones. An input of ten thousand values requires one hundred million steps, and an input of one million values requires one trillion steps, which is far beyond what we would wait for.

Cubic time, written big O of n cubed, arises when an algorithm considers every triple of values, or when it performs a linear amount of work for every pair. The textbook algorithm for multiplying two n by n matrices is cubic, because each of the n squared entries in the result is computed by a sum of n products. Some algorithms on graphs that work with every pair of vertices and consider every possible intermediate vertex are also cubic. Cubic time is noticeably worse than quadratic; for an input of one thousand values it requires one billion steps, which a modern computer can manage, but for ten thousand values it requires one trillion steps.

All the rates discussed so far are called polynomial rates, because each is bounded by n raised to some fixed power. Beyond polynomial rates lie the exponential rates, and here the behavior changes dramatically. An exponential algorithm, written big O of two to the power of n, doubles its running time every time the input grows by one. An input of ten values requires about one thousand steps, an input of twenty requires about one million, an input of thirty requires about one billion, and an input of sixty requires about one quintillion, which is more steps than any computer could perform in a human lifetime. Exponential algorithms typically arise when a problem seems to require trying every subset of the input, since a set of n values has two to the power of n subsets. Many important problems have no known algorithm better than exponential, and a later section of this book discusses what that means.

Even worse than exponential is factorial time, written big O of n factorial. The factorial of n is the product of all the whole numbers from one to n, and it is the number of ways to arrange n distinct values in a line. An algorithm that tries every arrangement of its input runs in factorial time. The factorial of ten is about three million six hundred thousand, the factorial of fifteen is about one trillion, and the factorial of twenty is about two quintillion. Factorial time is hopeless for anything but tiny inputs. The brute force approach to the traveling salesman problem, which tries every possible order of visiting the cities, is a well known example of a factorial algorithm.

It is worth pausing to appreciate the ordering of these rates. Constant is faster than logarithmic, which is faster than linear, which is faster than n log n, which is faster than quadratic, which is faster than cubic, which is faster than exponential, which is faster than factorial. The gaps between adjacent rates in this list are not all the same size. The gap between linear and n log n is small, and in practice an n log n algorithm feels almost as fast as a linear one. The gap between polynomial and exponential, on the other hand, is enormous, and it is the most important dividing line in the entire subject. An algorithm with polynomial running time is usually regarded as practical, at least in principle, while an algorithm with exponential running time is usually regarded as impractical for all but the smallest inputs.

So far we have spoken as though the running time of an algorithm depends only on the size of the input, but this is not quite true. Two inputs of the same size can lead to very different amounts of work. Consider searching an unsorted list of n values for a particular target value by examining the values one at a time from the beginning. If the target happens to be the first value, the algorithm stops after one comparison. If the target is the last value, or if it is not in the list at all, the algorithm performs n comparisons. The size of the input is n in both cases, but the running time ranges from one step to n steps.

To handle this, we distinguish between the best case, the worst case, and the average case. The best case running time is the smallest number of steps the algorithm performs on any input of size n. The worst case running time is the largest number of steps it performs on any input of size n. The average case running time is the expected number of steps over all inputs of size n, assuming some probability distribution over those inputs, usually the uniform distribution in which every input is equally likely. For the linear search just described, the best case is constant time, the worst case is linear time, and the average case is also linear time, since on average the target is found about halfway through the list.

Of these three, the worst case is the one most often reported, for several reasons. The worst case is a guarantee: whatever input arrives, the algorithm will take no longer than the worst case bound. Such guarantees matter for systems that must respond within a fixed time. The worst case is also usually the easiest to analyze, because we need only find the input that causes the most work. The average case, by contrast, requires us to choose a probability distribution over inputs, and that choice is often arbitrary; the inputs a program actually receives in practice may look nothing like uniformly random inputs. Finally, for many algorithms the worst case and the average case have the same growth rate, so analyzing the worst case tells us what we want to know about typical behavior as well.

There is one famous algorithm for which the average case and the worst case differ by a whole growth rate. Quicksort runs in n log n time on average but in quadratic time in the worst case. The worst case arises when the algorithm repeatedly chooses a poor pivot value, such as the smallest or largest value remaining, so that each step of the algorithm removes only one value from consideration. On a random input this is extremely unlikely, and for that reason quicksort is used widely despite its poor worst case. Some implementations choose the pivot at random, which makes the bad case unlikely regardless of the input, so that the average case analysis applies to every input. This is an example of how the choice of case can change which algorithm looks best.

Running time is not the only resource an algorithm consumes. An algorithm also uses memory, and the amount of memory it uses as a function of the input size is called its space complexity. We measure space in the same way we measure time, by counting the number of memory cells the algorithm needs beyond the input itself, and we describe the result using the same asymptotic notation. An algorithm that uses a fixed number of variables regardless of input size has constant space complexity. An algorithm that makes a copy of its input has linear space complexity. An algorithm that builds a table with an entry for every pair of input values has quadratic space complexity.

The largest value algorithm uses constant space, since it needs only one variable for the record and one for the current position. Merge sort, by contrast, uses linear space, because it creates temporary lists to hold the values being merged. Some sorting algorithms, such as heap sort, achieve n log n time while using only constant extra space, and this is one of their advantages. Space and time can often be traded against each other: an algorithm can be made faster by remembering results in a table so that they need not be recomputed, at the cost of the memory the table occupies. Choosing the right balance depends on the problem and on the machine.

We now turn to a more subtle kind of analysis, called amortized analysis, which is best introduced through the example of the dynamic array. A dynamic array is a list that supports adding a value to the end, and that grows as needed to accommodate new values. Internally, a dynamic array keeps a block of memory with some capacity, together with a count of how many values are currently stored. When the count is less than the capacity, adding a value is constant time: the algorithm writes the value into the next free cell and increments the count. The trouble arises when the count equals the capacity and there is no free cell.

When the array is full, the algorithm must allocate a new block of memory, copy every existing value into the new block, and then add the new value. If the array currently holds n values, this copying takes linear time. So an individual add operation can take either constant time or linear time, depending on whether the array happens to be full. If we analyzed the dynamic array using worst case analysis on each operation in isolation, we would conclude that adding a value is a linear time operation, and that adding n values one after another takes quadratic time in total. This conclusion is correct as an upper bound, but it is wildly pessimistic, and amortized analysis explains why.

The key design decision is how much to grow the array when it fills up. If we grow it by one cell each time, every add operation after the first few requires a full copy, and the quadratic bound is tight. If instead we double the capacity each time the array fills, the expensive copy operations become rare. After a copy that produces a block of capacity two n, the next n add operations are all constant time, because there are n free cells. Only when those n cells are used up does another copy occur, and that copy costs about two n steps. So for every expensive operation, there is a long stretch of cheap operations that precedes it.

Let us count the total cost of adding n values to an empty dynamic array that doubles its capacity when full. The cheap part of each add is constant, so the cheap parts cost n steps in total. The expensive parts are the copies, which occur when the count reaches one, two, four, eight, and so on up to the largest power of two not exceeding n. The copy at size k costs about k steps. Summing k over all the powers of two up to n gives a total less than two n, because each term in the sum is at most twice the previous term and the sum of such a sequence is at most twice its largest term. So the total cost of all the copies is at most two n, and the total cost of all n add operations is at most three n, which is linear.

Since n operations together cost at most three n steps, we say that each operation costs constant time on an amortized basis. The word amortized is borrowed from finance, where it refers to spreading the cost of a large expense across many payments. In amortized analysis we spread the cost of the occasional expensive operation across the many cheap operations that surround it. No single add operation is guaranteed to be fast, but any sequence of n add operations is guaranteed to take linear time in total, and for most purposes that guarantee is what we need.

We now turn to the analysis of recursive algorithms, which requires a new tool. A recursive algorithm solves a problem by calling itself on smaller instances of the same problem, and then combining the results. The running time of such an algorithm is naturally described by an equation that expresses the time for an input of size n in terms of the time for smaller inputs. Such an equation is called a recurrence relation, or simply a recurrence. Solving the recurrence means finding a closed form expression, or at least an asymptotic bound, for the running time as a function of n.

Consider binary search once more. On an input of size n, binary search performs one comparison and then calls itself on an input of size about n over two. If we write T of n for the running time on an input of size n, we obtain the recurrence T of n equals T of n over two plus a constant, with the base case that T of one is a constant. Unrolling this recurrence, we see that T of n equals T of n over four plus two constants, then T of n over eight plus three constants, and so on. After log n levels of unrolling we reach the base case, having added log n constants, so T of n is big Theta of log n, which matches the intuitive argument given earlier.

Merge sort gives a recurrence of a different shape. On an input of size n, merge sort splits the input into two halves, sorts each half recursively, and then merges the two sorted halves in linear time. So T of n equals two times T of n over two plus a constant times n. Here the recursive part doubles the number of subproblems while halving their size, and the nonrecursive part does linear work. Unrolling this recurrence is a little harder than unrolling the binary search recurrence, and it helps to have a systematic method.

The systematic method is called the recursion tree. We draw the original problem as the root of a tree, and we write beside it the nonrecursive work done at that level, which for merge sort is a constant times n. Below the root we draw one child for each recursive call; merge sort makes two calls on inputs of size n over two, so the root has two children, each labeled with nonrecursive work of a constant times n over two. Each of those children has two children of its own, with inputs of size n over four, and so on down to the leaves, which are inputs of size one. The total running time is the sum of the work written at every node of the tree.

The recursion tree makes the sum easy to organize level by level. At the root level there is one node doing a constant times n work. At the next level there are two nodes, each doing a constant times n over two, for a total of a constant times n. At the level below there are four nodes, each doing a constant times n over four, again a constant times n in total. Every level of the tree does the same total work, a constant times n, and the tree has log n levels, because the input size halves at each level until it reaches one. So the total work is a constant times n log n, and merge sort runs in big Theta of n log n time.

Other recurrences produce trees with different shapes. If each level does less total work than the level above, the sum is dominated by the root, and the running time is just the nonrecursive work at the top. If each level does more total work than the level above, the sum is dominated by the leaves, and the running time is proportional to the number of leaves. If every level does the same work, as in merge sort, the running time is the work per level multiplied by the number of levels. These three situations occur so often that there is a general result, called the master theorem, which covers all of them at once.

The master theorem applies to recurrences of the form T of n equals a times T of n over b plus f of n, where a is the number of subproblems, b is the factor by which the input shrinks, and f of n is the nonrecursive work. The theorem compares f of n with a particular critical function, namely n raised to the power log base b of a, which is the number of leaves in the recursion tree. If f of n grows polynomially more slowly than the critical function, the leaves dominate, and the running time is big Theta of the critical function. If f of n grows at the same rate as the critical function, every level contributes equally, and the running time is big Theta of the critical function times log n. If f of n grows polynomially faster than the critical function, and a mild regularity condition holds, the root dominates, and the running time is big Theta of f of n.

For merge sort, a is two and b is two, so the critical function is n to the power log base two of two, which is n to the first power, which is n. The nonrecursive work f of n is also a constant times n, so we are in the middle case, and the running time is big Theta of n log n. For binary search, a is one and b is two, so the critical function is n to the power zero, which is one. The nonrecursive work is constant, again matching the critical function, so the running time is big Theta of log n. The master theorem is a convenient shortcut, but the recursion tree is what makes it true, and it is worth being able to draw the tree whenever the theorem does not apply.

We close this section with the subject of lower bounds, which turn the question around. Instead of asking how fast a particular algorithm is, we ask how fast any algorithm for a given problem could possibly be. The most celebrated result of this kind concerns sorting. We have seen that merge sort and heap sort run in n log n time. Could some cleverer algorithm sort in linear time? The answer, for a large and natural class of algorithms, is no.

The class in question consists of comparison sorts, which are algorithms that learn about the input only by comparing pairs of values and asking which is larger. Merge sort, heap sort, quicksort, insertion sort, and every other sorting algorithm discussed so far is a comparison sort. The argument proceeds by counting. An input of n distinct values can appear in any of n factorial orders, and a correct sorting algorithm must behave differently on each of them, because each requires a different rearrangement. Each comparison has two possible outcomes, so after k comparisons the algorithm can have distinguished at most two to the power of k different situations. To distinguish all n factorial orders, the algorithm needs two to the power of k to be at least n factorial, which means k must be at least the logarithm of n factorial.

The logarithm of n factorial is big Theta of n log n, a fact that follows from a standard estimate of the size of factorials. So any comparison sort must perform at least a constant times n log n comparisons in the worst case, and its running time is big Omega of n log n. This is a statement about every possible comparison sort, including ones nobody has invented yet. Since merge sort achieves big O of n log n, its upper bound matches the lower bound for the problem, and we say that merge sort is asymptotically optimal among comparison sorts. No comparison based algorithm can beat it by more than a constant factor.

The lower bound does not forbid faster sorting by algorithms that do something other than compare. Counting sort and radix sort inspect the digits or the numeric values of the input directly, rather than comparing pairs, and under suitable assumptions about the range of the values they run in linear time. These algorithms do not contradict the lower bound; they escape it by working outside the model in which the bound was proved. This illustrates a general lesson about lower bounds: every lower bound is proved within some model of computation, and it tells us what is impossible within that model. Changing the model can change what is possible.


Every later chapter of this book rests on a small collection of mathematical ideas that computer scientists use every day without always naming them. These ideas are called discrete mathematics, because they deal with objects that come in separate, countable pieces rather than in smooth continuous quantities. A computer stores a finite number of bits, runs a finite number of steps, and manipulates a finite collection of values, so the mathematics of discrete objects is the natural language for describing what a computer does. This section introduces the central tools: sets, functions, relations, logic, proof, counting, probability, number theory, and graphs. None of these topics is difficult on its own, but each one supplies a vocabulary that the rest of the book assumes you already speak.

We begin with sets, because almost every other object in this section is built from them. A set is a collection of distinct elements, and the only question a set answers is whether a given element belongs to it or not. The set of even numbers contains the number four and does not contain the number seven. The order in which we list the elements of a set does not matter, and listing an element twice does not change the set. We say that an element x is a member of a set S when x belongs to S, and we say that a set A is a subset of a set B when every element of A is also an element of B. The empty set is the set with no elements at all, and it is a subset of every set, because there is no element of the empty set that could fail to be in the other set. A set is finite when it has a finite number of elements, and the number of elements in a finite set is called its size or its cardinality.

The basic operations on sets combine two sets into a third. The union of A and B is the set of elements that belong to A or to B or to both. The intersection of A and B is the set of elements that belong to both A and B at the same time. The difference of A and B is the set of elements that belong to A but not to B. The complement of a set A, relative to some larger universe of discourse, is the set of elements in that universe that are not in A. These operations obey familiar laws: union and intersection are each commutative and associative, and each distributes over the other. A particularly useful pair of laws, named after De Morgan, says that the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements. These laws will reappear in propositional logic in a few paragraphs, which is no accident, because the logic of true and false statements and the algebra of sets are two faces of the same structure.

Two more constructions deserve mention. The power set of a set S is the set of all subsets of S, including the empty set and S itself. If S has n elements, then its power set has two to the power of n elements, because each element can either be included or excluded from a subset, and these n independent choices multiply together. The Cartesian product of two sets A and B is the set of all ordered pairs whose first component comes from A and whose second component comes from B. If A has m elements and B has n elements, then the product has m times n ordered pairs. The Cartesian product is the foundation for relations and functions, because both of those objects are, at bottom, sets of ordered pairs.

A relation from a set A to a set B is simply a subset of the Cartesian product of A and B. We say that an element a is related to an element b when the ordered pair of a and b belongs to the relation. A relation on a single set A is a subset of the product of A with itself. The relation less than on the integers contains the pair of three and five but not the pair of five and three. Relations on a single set are often classified by three properties. A relation is reflexive when every element is related to itself. A relation is symmetric when, whenever a is related to b, it follows that b is related to a. A relation is transitive when, whenever a is related to b and b is related to c, it follows that a is related to c. A relation that has all three properties is called an equivalence relation, and an equivalence relation splits its set into disjoint pieces called equivalence classes, where two elements lie in the same class exactly when they are related. Equivalence relations appear throughout computer science, from the states of a machine that behave identically to the integers that leave the same remainder when divided by a fixed number.

A function is a special kind of relation. A function from a set A to a set B assigns to every element of A exactly one element of B. The set A is called the domain, and the set B is called the codomain. The element of B assigned to a is called the image of a, and we write it as f of a. The key restriction is the word exactly: every element of the domain gets one image, never zero and never two. A function is called injective, or one to one, when distinct elements of the domain always have distinct images. A function is called surjective, or onto, when every element of the codomain is the image of at least one element of the domain. A function that is both injective and surjective is called a bijection, and a bijection between two finite sets shows that they have the same number of elements. This last fact is the reason bijections matter so much in counting arguments: if you want to know how many objects of one kind there are, it is often easiest to match them one to one with objects of a kind you already know how to count.

We turn now to logic, which is the study of statements and the rules by which true statements can be combined to yield other true statements. A proposition is a statement that is either true or false, but not both. The sentence seven is a prime number is a proposition, and it happens to be true. The sentence this sentence is false is not a proposition in the usual sense, because it cannot consistently be assigned either truth value. Propositional logic studies how propositions combine using connectives. The connective and produces a statement that is true only when both parts are true. The connective or produces a statement that is true when at least one part is true. The connective not flips a statement from true to false or from false to true. The connective if then, called implication, produces a statement that is false only when the first part is true and the second part is false; in every other case the implication is true, including the case where the first part is false.

The truth value of a compound statement depends only on the truth values of its parts, and a truth table lists every combination. Two compound statements are logically equivalent when they have the same truth value under every assignment to their parts. For example, the statement if p then q is logically equivalent to the statement if not q then not p, which is called its contrapositive. On the other hand, if p then q is not equivalent to its converse, which is if q then p, and confusing a statement with its converse is one of the most common errors in informal reasoning. De Morgan's laws hold here just as they did for sets: not the statement p and q is equivalent to not p or not q, and not the statement p or q is equivalent to not p and not q. A statement that is true under every assignment is called a tautology, and a statement that is false under every assignment is called a contradiction.

Propositional logic cannot express a sentence such as every integer has a successor, because that sentence talks about all the elements of a set at once. Predicate logic adds variables and quantifiers to fix this gap. A predicate is a statement whose truth depends on the value of one or more variables, such as x is even. The universal quantifier, read as for all, asserts that a predicate holds for every element of the domain. The existential quantifier, read as there exists, asserts that the predicate holds for at least one element. The order of quantifiers matters enormously. The statement for every number x there exists a number y greater than x is true, because we can choose y after seeing x. The statement there exists a number y such that for every number x, y is greater than x is false, because no single number is larger than all the others. Negating a quantified statement swaps the quantifier: the negation of for all x, P of x is there exists x such that not P of x, and the negation of there exists x such that P of x is for all x, not P of x. These rules tell us exactly what it takes to disprove a universal claim, namely a single counterexample, and what it takes to disprove an existential claim, namely a proof that every candidate fails.

With logic in hand, we can say what a proof is. A proof is a finite sequence of statements, each of which is either an assumption, a previously established fact, or a statement that follows from earlier statements in the sequence by an accepted rule of inference. The final statement in the sequence is the theorem being proved. The most basic rule of inference, called modus ponens, says that from p and from if p then q we may conclude q. Every proof, however long and clever, is ultimately a chain of such small steps. A proof is not an argument that seems convincing; it is an argument that could, in principle, be checked mechanically by someone who understands the rules but has no intuition at all. This standard is what allows computer scientists to trust that an algorithm is correct, that a protocol is secure, or that a problem cannot be solved, and it is the same standard that makes automated proof checking possible.

The simplest proof strategy is direct proof. To prove a statement of the form if p then q by direct proof, we assume that p is true and then reason, step by step, until we arrive at q. As an example, let us prove that the sum of two even integers is even. Assume that a and b are even integers. By the definition of even, there exist integers j and k such that a equals two times j and b equals two times k. Then a plus b equals two times j plus two times k, which equals two times the quantity j plus k. Since j plus k is an integer, the sum a plus b is two times an integer, and so it is even by definition. That is the whole proof. Notice that it begins by unpacking a definition, performs a short computation, and ends by repacking the result into the definition of the conclusion. The great majority of direct proofs have exactly this shape.

Proof by contradiction takes a different route. To prove that a statement p is true, we assume instead that p is false, and we derive from this assumption some statement that is known to be false, such as a statement of the form q and not q. Since a true assumption cannot lead to a false conclusion by valid steps, the assumption that p is false must itself be false, and therefore p is true. The classic example is the proof that the square root of two is not a rational number. Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that the square root of two equals a over b, where a and b are positive integers with no common factor. Squaring both sides and clearing the denominator gives a squared equals two times b squared, so a squared is even, and therefore a is even, because the square of an odd number is odd. Write a as two times c. Then four times c squared equals two times b squared, so b squared equals two times c squared, and b is even by the same reasoning. But now a and b are both even, which contradicts our assumption that they share no common factor. Hence the square root of two is irrational. Proof by contradiction is the natural tool whenever the thing to be proved is a negative statement, such as a claim that something does not exist or cannot be done, and the book will use it repeatedly when it shows that certain problems have no algorithm at all.

Proof by induction is the technique that computer scientists use more than any other, because it matches the way programs loop and the way data structures nest. Induction proves that a statement P of n holds for every natural number n by establishing two things. The base case shows that P of zero, or P of one, is true. The inductive step shows that for every natural number k, if P of k is true then P of k plus one is also true. The assumption that P of k is true inside the inductive step is called the inductive hypothesis. Together the two parts cover every natural number, because the base case gives us the first value, the inductive step carries us from the first value to the second, from the second to the third, and so on without end. Induction is valid because the natural numbers are built by starting at zero and repeatedly adding one, and a property that survives each step survives forever.

The standard first example is the formula for the sum of the first n positive integers. The claim is that the sum of one through n equals n times n plus one over two. For the base case, when n is one, the sum is one and the formula gives one times two over two, which is also one. For the inductive step, assume that the sum of one through k equals k times k plus one over two. Then the sum of one through k plus one is that sum plus the next term, which is k times k plus one over two, plus k plus one. Factoring out k plus one gives k plus one times the quantity k over two plus one, which equals k plus one times k plus two over two. This is exactly the formula with k plus one in place of k, so the inductive step is complete, and the formula holds for every positive integer n. The same pattern proves that the sum of the first n odd numbers equals n squared, and that the sum of the powers of two from one up to two to the power of n equals two to the power of n plus one, minus one.

Induction is not limited to numbers. Any structure that is defined recursively, by giving some base objects and some rules for building bigger objects from smaller ones, admits a form of induction called structural induction. A binary tree is either empty or consists of a root node together with a left subtree and a right subtree, each of which is a binary tree. To prove a statement about all binary trees, we prove it for the empty tree and then prove that, if it holds for both subtrees of some node, it holds for the tree rooted at that node. For instance, we can prove that a binary tree with n nodes has exactly n plus one empty subtrees. The empty tree has zero nodes and one empty subtree, so the base case holds. For a nonempty tree, suppose the left subtree has a nodes and a plus one empty subtrees, and the right subtree has b nodes and b plus one empty subtrees. The whole tree then has a plus b plus one nodes and a plus b plus two empty subtrees, which is again one more than the number of nodes. Structural induction is the tool for reasoning about linked lists, expression trees, grammars, and the recursive functions that process them, and it will be used heavily when the book discusses formal languages and automata.

The pigeonhole principle is a proof tool so simple that it hardly seems worth naming, yet it produces surprising conclusions. It states that if n plus one or more objects are placed into n boxes, then at least one box contains two or more objects. The proof is a short contradiction: if every box held at most one object, the total number of objects would be at most n, contrary to assumption. A generalized form says that if m objects are placed into n boxes, then some box contains at least the ceiling of m over n objects. From this principle it follows that in any group of thirteen people, two share a birth month, and that any function from a larger finite set to a smaller finite set must send two different elements to the same image, so it cannot be injective. That last consequence has deep implications for computer science: no lossless compression scheme can shrink every file, because there are more files of a given length than there are shorter files to map them to. The pigeonhole principle also underlies the proof that any finite automaton processing a sufficiently long input must repeat a state, which is the heart of the pumping lemma in a later section.

Counting is the next tool, and it begins with two rules. The sum rule says that if a task can be done in one of a ways or in one of b ways, and the two sets of ways do not overlap, then there are a plus b ways to do it. The product rule says that if a task consists of two independent steps, the first with a choices and the second with b choices, then there are a times b ways to perform both steps. The product rule explains why a binary string of length n has two to the power of n possibilities, and why a password of length eight drawn from an alphabet of sixty-two characters has sixty-two to the power of eight possibilities. When the sets in the sum rule do overlap, we correct by subtracting the overlap, which is the principle of inclusion and exclusion: the size of the union of A and B equals the size of A plus the size of B minus the size of their intersection.

A permutation is an arrangement of objects in a definite order. The number of ways to arrange n distinct objects in a row is n factorial, which is the product of all the integers from one up to n, because there are n choices for the first position, n minus one for the second, and so on down to one. The number of ways to choose and arrange k objects out of n is n factorial divided by n minus k factorial, often called the number of k permutations of n. A combination, by contrast, is a selection of objects in which order does not matter. The number of ways to choose k objects from n without regard to order is written n choose k and equals n factorial divided by the product of k factorial and n minus k factorial. The division by k factorial removes the overcounting caused by the k factorial different orderings of the same selected set. For example, five choose two equals ten, because there are twenty ordered pairs of distinct elements from a set of five, and each unordered pair appears twice.

Counting leads naturally to probability, because in the simplest setting a probability is a ratio of counts. A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment, and an event is a subset of the sample space. When every outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in the event divided by the number of outcomes in the sample space. The probability of rolling an even number on a fair six sided die is three over six, which is one half. Probabilities lie between zero and one, the probability of the whole sample space is one, and the probability of a union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities. The probability that an event does not occur is one minus the probability that it does, which is often the easier quantity to compute.

Two events are independent when the probability that both occur equals the product of their individual probabilities. The conditional probability of A given B is the probability that both A and B occur divided by the probability of B, and it describes how our estimate of A should change once we learn that B happened. A random variable is a function from the sample space to the real numbers, such as the number of heads in ten coin flips, and its expected value is the sum, over all possible values, of each value times the probability of that value. Expected value is linear: the expected value of a sum of random variables equals the sum of their expected values, even when the variables are not independent. This linearity is the single most useful fact in the analysis of randomized algorithms, because it lets us compute the expected running time of a procedure by adding up the expected cost of each step without worrying about how the steps interact. Hashing, randomized quicksort, and probabilistic primality testing are all analyzed this way, and the birthday problem, which asks how many people must gather before two probably share a birthday, is a direct application of the pigeonhole principle combined with a probability computation.

Modular arithmetic is the arithmetic of remainders, and it is the number system that computers actually use for integers. We say that a is congruent to b modulo m when m divides the difference of a and b, or equivalently when a and b leave the same remainder upon division by m. The relation of congruence modulo m is an equivalence relation on the integers, and its equivalence classes are the m residue classes, one for each possible remainder from zero up to m minus one. Congruence respects addition and multiplication: if a is congruent to b modulo m and c is congruent to d modulo m, then a plus c is congruent to b plus d modulo m, and a times c is congruent to b times d modulo m. This means we may reduce intermediate results modulo m at any point in a computation without changing the final answer modulo m, which is exactly what a machine does when it stores integers in a fixed number of bits and discards any overflow.

Division in modular arithmetic is more delicate than addition and multiplication. An integer a has a multiplicative inverse modulo m, meaning an integer b with a times b congruent to one modulo m, exactly when a and m have no common factor other than one. When m is a prime number p, every nonzero residue has an inverse, and the residues modulo p form a field in which we can add, subtract, multiply, and divide freely. Fermat's little theorem states that if p is prime and a is not divisible by p, then a to the power of p minus one is congruent to one modulo p. This theorem gives a fast way to compute modular inverses and modular powers, and it is the basis of the Miller Rabin primality test and of the RSA public key system, both of which the book treats in a later section. Modular exponentiation by repeated squaring lets us compute a to the power of e modulo m using only about the logarithm of e multiplications, which is why cryptographic operations on numbers with hundreds of digits are practical at all.

A prime number is an integer greater than one whose only positive divisors are one and itself. The first few primes are two, three, five, seven, eleven, and thirteen. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every integer greater than one can be written as a product of primes, and that this factorization is unique except for the order of the factors. The existence half of this theorem is proved by induction on n, using the fact that a number which is not prime is a product of two smaller numbers. Euclid proved that there are infinitely many primes by contradiction: suppose there were only finitely many, multiply them all together and add one, and observe that the resulting number is divisible by none of the listed primes, yet it must have some prime factor, which is a contradiction. The distribution of primes is irregular in detail but regular on average, and the prime number theorem says that the number of primes up to n is roughly n divided by the natural logarithm of n, which guarantees that primes with any desired number of digits are plentiful enough to find by random search.

The greatest common divisor of two integers a and b, written gcd of a and b, is the largest positive integer that divides both of them. Two integers are called relatively prime, or coprime, when their greatest common divisor is one. The Euclidean algorithm computes the greatest common divisor without factoring either number, by repeatedly replacing the larger number with its remainder upon division by the smaller, until the remainder is zero; the last nonzero remainder is the answer. The correctness of the algorithm rests on the observation that any common divisor of a and b is also a common divisor of b and the remainder of a divided by b, so the greatest common divisor is unchanged by each step. The algorithm is remarkably fast, taking a number of steps proportional to the number of digits of the inputs. The extended Euclidean algorithm additionally finds integers x and y such that a times x plus b times y equals the greatest common divisor of a and b, a result known as Bezout's identity, and this is precisely how multiplicative inverses modulo m are computed in practice. Number theory of this kind was once considered the purest and least applicable branch of mathematics, and it is now the backbone of every secure connection on the internet.

The last tool in this section is graph theory, which supplies the vocabulary for networks, data structures, state machines, and dependencies of every kind. A graph consists of a set of vertices together with a set of edges, where each edge joins two vertices. In an undirected graph an edge is an unordered pair of vertices, so the edge between u and v is the same as the edge between v and u. In a directed graph an edge is an ordered pair, and the edge from u to v is different from the edge from v to u. Two vertices joined by an edge are called adjacent, and the degree of a vertex in an undirected graph is the number of edges touching it. A simple counting argument shows that the sum of the degrees of all the vertices equals twice the number of edges, because every edge contributes one to the degree of each of its two endpoints. It follows immediately that the number of vertices with odd degree is even, which is a small but genuine theorem proved by pure counting.

A path in a graph is a sequence of vertices in which each consecutive pair is joined by an edge, and the length of the path is the number of edges it uses. A path is simple when it visits no vertex more than once. A cycle is a path of length at least three that begins and ends at the same vertex and otherwise visits distinct vertices. A graph is connected when there is a path between every pair of vertices, and the connected components of a graph are its maximal connected pieces. The relation of being joined by a path is an equivalence relation on the vertices, and its equivalence classes are exactly the connected components, which gives yet another place where the abstract idea of an equivalence relation does concrete work. In a directed graph, we distinguish strong connectivity, where every vertex can reach every other along directed edges, from weak connectivity, which ignores edge directions. The distance between two vertices is the length of a shortest path between them, and computing distances is one of the first graph algorithms the book will study.

A tree is a connected undirected graph with no cycles. Trees have several equivalent characterizations, and proving that they are equivalent is a good exercise in the proof techniques of this section. A graph with n vertices is a tree exactly when it is connected and has n minus one edges, exactly when it has no cycles and has n minus one edges, and exactly when there is a unique simple path between every pair of vertices. The proof that a tree with n vertices has n minus one edges goes by induction on n: a tree with one vertex has zero edges, and a tree with more than one vertex has a vertex of degree one, called a leaf, whose removal yields a smaller tree with one fewer vertex and one fewer edge. A rooted tree singles out one vertex as the root, after which every other vertex has a unique parent on the path toward the root, and the binary trees that appeared in the discussion of structural induction are rooted trees in which each vertex has at most two children.

A graph is bipartite when its vertex set can be split into two parts such that every edge joins a vertex in one part to a vertex in the other, with no edge inside a single part. Bipartite graphs model matching problems, such as assigning workers to jobs or students to courses, where edges indicate which pairings are allowed. A graph is bipartite exactly when it contains no cycle of odd length, and the proof of this characterization is a nice combination of the tools in this section: one direction is a direct argument that alternates colors along a cycle, and the other direction colors each vertex according to the parity of its distance from a chosen starting vertex and uses contradiction to show that no edge joins two vertices of the same color. Every tree is bipartite, because a tree has no cycles at all. Checking whether a graph is bipartite is a simple application of the breadth first search algorithm that appears later in the book.

Why do these particular tools underlie everything that follows? Sets and functions give us the language in which every definition in the book is written: a language is a set of strings, an automaton is a function from states and symbols to states, and a decision problem is a function from inputs to yes or no. Logic tells us what a statement means and what counts as establishing it, and the proof techniques of this section are the only means by which the later theorems can be known rather than merely believed. Induction is the tool for reasoning about loops, recursion, and every structure built from smaller copies of itself. The pigeonhole principle and counting arguments show that certain things are impossible, which is often the deepest kind of result in the subject. Probability gives us the means to analyze algorithms that flip coins and to understand hashing and randomized testing. Modular arithmetic and number theory are the raw material of cryptography and of the integer arithmetic every processor performs. Graphs are the universal model of structure and connection. When a later section states a theorem, the proof will draw on these tools without comment, and the reader who has mastered them will find that most of the hard work was already done here.


Every computer program, at the lowest level, is a machine that reads symbols and reacts to them. The theory of automata and formal languages studies the simplest such machines and asks a precise question about each one: which sets of strings can this kind of machine recognize? The answer turns out to organize a great deal of computer science, from the design of text search tools to the construction of compilers. In this section we build the theory from the ground up, starting with alphabets and strings, moving through finite automata and regular expressions, then climbing to context-free grammars and pushdown automata, and finally placing all of these models in the ladder known as the Chomsky hierarchy.

An alphabet is a finite, nonempty set of symbols. The symbols themselves are not important; what matters is that we can tell them apart. A common alphabet is the one containing just the two symbols zero and one. Another is the set of lowercase letters of the English alphabet. In a programming language, the alphabet might be the full set of characters a keyboard can produce. Once we fix an alphabet, a string over that alphabet is a finite sequence of symbols drawn from it. The string zero one one zero has length four. The string with no symbols at all is called the empty string, and it has length zero. The empty string is a genuine string, just as zero is a genuine number, and we will see that it plays an important role in many definitions.

A language over an alphabet is simply a set of strings over that alphabet. The set may be finite or infinite. The language of all strings over zero and one that have an even number of zeros is an infinite language. The language containing only the three strings cat, dog, and bird is a finite language. The empty language, which contains no strings at all, is also a language, and it should not be confused with the language that contains only the empty string. The first has no members; the second has exactly one member.

Why describe computational problems as languages? Because any decision problem, meaning any question with a yes or no answer, can be recast as a language. The set of all inputs for which the answer is yes forms a language, and solving the problem is the same as deciding membership in that language. A machine that reads an input string and announces accept or reject is therefore a machine that defines a language: the language of all strings it accepts. This single idea lets us compare wildly different machines on an equal footing. We do not ask what a machine computes in general; we ask which language it recognizes, and we compare machines by comparing the classes of languages they can recognize.

The simplest machine worth studying is the deterministic finite automaton. A deterministic finite automaton has a finite set of states, one of which is designated the start state, and some of which are designated accept states. It also has a transition function. For each state and each input symbol, the transition function names exactly one next state. The machine operates by reading its input string one symbol at a time from left to right. It begins in the start state. Each time it reads a symbol, it consults the transition function and moves to the indicated next state. When the input is exhausted, the machine looks at the state it is in. If that state is an accept state, the machine accepts the string; otherwise it rejects the string. The language recognized by the automaton is the set of all strings it accepts.

Consider a machine that decides whether a binary string has an even number of zeros. It needs only two states. Call them even and odd. The start state is even, since before reading any symbols the count of zeros is zero, which is even. The state even is also the only accept state. On reading the symbol one, the machine stays in whatever state it is in, because ones do not affect the count. On reading the symbol zero, the machine flips: even goes to odd and odd goes to even. After reading the entire input, the machine is in state even exactly when the number of zeros read was even. This small example captures the essence of the model. The machine has no memory beyond its current state, and its finite set of states is fixed before any input arrives.

That last point deserves emphasis. A deterministic finite automaton has a fixed, finite amount of memory, namely the identity of its current state. It cannot count without bound, it cannot look back at earlier symbols, and it cannot write anything down. Whatever it needs to remember about the input so far must be compressed into the choice of one state out of a finite set. This is a severe restriction, and yet the class of languages such machines recognize is surprisingly rich. We call these languages the regular languages, and much of the first half of this section is devoted to understanding exactly what they can and cannot do.

The word deterministic in the name refers to the fact that at every moment the machine has exactly one possible next state. There is no choice to be made. The transition function is a true function: given the current state and the current symbol, the next state is uniquely determined. A natural generalization is to allow choice. A nondeterministic finite automaton is like a deterministic one, except that from a given state on a given symbol, the machine may have several possible next states, or none at all. In addition, the machine may have special transitions that it can take without reading any input symbol; these are called empty transitions. A nondeterministic machine accepts a string if there exists at least one sequence of choices that leads it to an accept state when the input is exhausted. If every sequence of choices fails, the machine rejects.

It can be helpful to imagine a nondeterministic automaton as running many copies of itself in parallel. Whenever the machine faces a choice, it splits into one copy for each option, and each copy continues independently. A copy that reaches a state with no transition on the current symbol simply dies. At the end of the input, the string is accepted if any surviving copy is in an accept state. Another way to picture nondeterminism is to think of the machine as guessing. At each choice point it guesses which transition to take, and it always guesses correctly if a correct guess exists.

Nondeterminism often makes machines much easier to design. Suppose we want to recognize the language of all binary strings whose third symbol from the end is a one. A nondeterministic machine can simply read symbols, staying in its start state, and at some point guess that the symbol it is about to read is the third from the end. It then checks that this symbol is a one, reads exactly two more symbols, and accepts if the input ends at that point. If the guess was wrong, that copy dies, but if there is a one in the third position from the end, some copy will guess correctly. The machine needs only four states. A deterministic machine for the same language needs eight states, because it must remember the last three symbols it has seen, and there are eight possible combinations.

This raises the central question about nondeterminism: does it add power? Can a nondeterministic finite automaton recognize a language that no deterministic finite automaton can recognize? The answer is no, and the proof is one of the most important constructions in the subject. It is called the subset construction. Given a nondeterministic machine, we build a deterministic machine whose states are sets of states of the original machine. The idea is to track all the copies at once. The start state of the new machine is the set containing the old start state together with everything reachable from it by empty transitions. From a set of states, on a given symbol, the new machine moves to the set of all states reachable from any member of the current set by reading that symbol and then following any empty transitions. A set is an accept state of the new machine if it contains at least one accept state of the old machine.

The new machine is deterministic because for each set and each symbol there is exactly one resulting set. It accepts exactly the strings the original accepted, because its current set is always precisely the set of states the original machine could be in after reading the input so far. If the original machine had some number of states, the new machine has at most two raised to that number of states, since that is the number of subsets. In practice many of those subsets are unreachable and can be discarded, but in the worst case the blowup is real; the third from the end example shows that eight states are needed where four sufficed. So nondeterminism does not add recognizing power, but it can add enormous convenience and compactness. Every language recognized by a nondeterministic finite automaton is regular, and every regular language is recognized by some nondeterministic finite automaton, because a deterministic machine is just a special case of a nondeterministic one.

We now have two equivalent ways of describing regular languages, both in terms of machines. There is a third way that looks nothing like a machine, and it is probably the one most programmers encounter first: the regular expression. A regular expression is an algebraic notation for building languages out of smaller languages. The simplest expressions describe the empty language, the language containing only the empty string, and the language containing a single one-symbol string for each symbol of the alphabet. From these building blocks, three operations build more complex expressions. The union of two expressions describes all strings in either language. The concatenation of two expressions describes all strings formed by taking a string from the first language followed by a string from the second. The star of an expression describes all strings formed by concatenating zero or more strings from its language, which always includes the empty string.

As an example, the expression for all binary strings with an even number of zeros can be built as follows. Think of such a string as a sequence of blocks, where each block consists of some ones, then a zero, then some ones, then another zero. The string may also end with some trailing ones, and it may contain no zeros at all. The expression is the star of the pattern one star zero one star zero, concatenated with one star. Reading this aloud makes clear how the operations compose: star of a block, then a tail of ones. Regular expressions are compact and readable, and programmers use them daily in text editors, search tools, and input validation.

The remarkable fact is that regular expressions and finite automata describe exactly the same class of languages. Every language described by a regular expression is recognized by some finite automaton, and every language recognized by a finite automaton is described by some regular expression. The first direction is proved by building automata inductively. For each basic expression we build a tiny nondeterministic machine with one or two states. For a union, we build a new start state with empty transitions into the start states of the two component machines. For a concatenation, we add empty transitions from the accept states of the first machine into the start state of the second, and we make only the accept states of the second machine accepting. For a star, we add empty transitions from the accept states back to the start state, and a new start state that is also an accept state so that the empty string is accepted. Nondeterminism and empty transitions make this construction clean, which is one of the practical reasons the nondeterministic model is worth having.

The other direction, from automaton to expression, is proved by gradually eliminating states. We allow the transitions of the machine to be labeled by regular expressions rather than single symbols, and we remove states one at a time. When we remove a state, every path that went through it, from some predecessor to some successor, is replaced by a direct transition labeled with the concatenation of the incoming expression, the star of any self loop on the removed state, and the outgoing expression. When only the start state and a single accept state remain, the labels on the remaining transitions combine into one regular expression for the whole language.

With all these tools, it might seem that any reasonable language is regular. It is not. The classic non-regular language is the set of all strings consisting of some number of the symbol a followed by the same number of the symbol b. In words, a to the n followed by b to the n, for every nonnegative integer n. Intuitively, to check this language a machine must count the a's and then count down with the b's, and counting without bound requires unbounded memory. A finite automaton has only finitely many states, so it cannot distinguish among infinitely many different counts. This intuition is correct, but intuition is not proof. The tool that turns the intuition into a rigorous argument is the pumping lemma for regular languages.

The pumping lemma says the following. If a language is regular, then there is a number, called the pumping length, with this property: every string in the language whose length is at least the pumping length can be split into three pieces, call them x, y, and z, such that y is not the empty string, the combined length of x and y is at most the pumping length, and for every nonnegative integer i, the string formed by x followed by i copies of y followed by z is also in the language. In other words, every sufficiently long string in a regular language contains a short nonempty piece near its beginning that can be repeated any number of times, including zero times, without leaving the language.

The proof is a pigeonhole argument. Take the pumping length to be the number of states in a deterministic automaton for the language. A string of at least that length causes the machine to visit at least that many plus one states, counting the start state, so some state must be visited twice within the first pumping length symbols. The piece y is the part of the input read between the first and second visits to that repeated state. Since the machine is in the same state before and after reading y, it will be in the same state after reading y any number of times, including zero times. The rest of the computation is unchanged, so acceptance is unchanged. That is the whole proof.

To use the lemma to show a language is not regular, we argue by contradiction. Suppose the language were regular. Then it has some pumping length. We choose a specific string in the language of at least that length, cleverly chosen so that any valid split leads to trouble. We then show that for every possible way of splitting that string into x, y, and z satisfying the conditions, some pumped string falls outside the language. This contradicts the lemma, so the language cannot be regular. The choice of string is the creative step, and the constraint that x and y together are short is often what makes the argument work.

For a to the n followed by b to the n, choose the string consisting of exactly pumping length many a's followed by the same number of b's. Since x and y together have length at most the pumping length, both x and y lie entirely within the block of a's. Thus y consists of one or more a's. Pumping y up to two copies yields a string with more a's than b's, which is not in the language. The contradiction shows that the language is not regular. The same technique shows that the language of palindromes is not regular, that the language of strings with equal numbers of zeros and ones is not regular, and that the language of balanced parentheses is not regular. This last one has practical teeth: no regular expression can check that parentheses in an arithmetic expression are balanced, which is why text editors that try to do this with regular expressions alone get it wrong.

Balanced parentheses, nested structures, and matching counts are everywhere in programming languages and in natural language, and finite automata cannot handle them. We need a more powerful model. The next step up is the context-free grammar. A grammar does not read a string and accept or reject it; instead it generates strings. A context-free grammar consists of a finite set of variables, also called nonterminals, a finite set of terminal symbols, which form the alphabet of the generated language, a designated start variable, and a finite set of rules, also called productions. Each rule has a single variable on its left side and a string of variables and terminals on its right side. The rule says that the variable on the left may be replaced by the string on the right.

A derivation begins with the start variable and repeatedly applies rules, each time replacing one variable in the current string with the right side of some rule for that variable, until no variables remain. The resulting string of terminals is said to be derived by the grammar, and the language of the grammar is the set of all terminal strings it can derive. The word context-free refers to the fact that a rule can be applied to a variable regardless of what surrounds it; the context does not matter. This is what makes the model tractable, and it is also what limits it.

The language a to the n followed by b to the n, which no finite automaton can recognize, has a two-rule grammar. Let the start variable be S. The first rule says S may be replaced by the terminal a, then S, then the terminal b. The second rule says S may be replaced by the empty string. Starting from S, applying the first rule three times and then the second rule yields three a's, then nothing, then three b's. Every derivation produces a string with the same number of a's as b's, with all a's first, and every such string can be derived. The grammar counts by nesting: each application of the first rule wraps the growing string in one more matched pair.

Balanced parentheses have an equally simple grammar. The start variable S may be replaced by the empty string, by an open parenthesis followed by S followed by a close parenthesis, or by S followed by S. The second rule produces nesting and the third produces sequencing. Arithmetic expressions with addition, multiplication, and parentheses have a grammar with variables for expression, term, and factor. An expression is a term or an expression plus a term. A term is a factor or a term times a factor. A factor is a number or a parenthesized expression. This grammar not only generates exactly the well-formed expressions; it also encodes the rule that multiplication binds more tightly than addition, because multiplication appears deeper in the structure.

That observation about structure brings us to parse trees. A derivation lists the order in which rules were applied, but the order is often irrelevant; what matters is which rule was applied to which occurrence of which variable. A parse tree captures exactly that. The root is the start variable. Each internal node is a variable, and its children, read left to right, are the symbols on the right side of the rule applied to it. The leaves, read left to right, spell out the derived string. Two derivations that differ only in the order of rule applications produce the same parse tree. For a compiler, the parse tree is the real product of parsing, because it shows that two plus three times four means two plus the product of three and four. Everything the compiler does afterward works on the tree rather than on the raw string.

This brings up a serious problem. What if a string has two different parse trees under the same grammar? A grammar in which some string has more than one parse tree is called ambiguous. Consider a naive expression grammar with a single variable E, where E may be replaced by E plus E, by E times E, or by a number. The string two plus three times four has two parse trees under this grammar: one in which the top node is the plus rule and the multiplication is a subtree, and one in which the top node is the times rule and the addition is a subtree. These two trees mean different things, one evaluating to fourteen and the other to twenty. A compiler built on this grammar would not know which meaning to choose.

Sometimes ambiguity can be removed by rewriting the grammar, as we did with the expression, term, and factor version, which forces the precedence structure into the shape of the tree. The classic dangling else problem in programming languages, in which an else clause could attach to either of two nested if statements, is another ambiguity that language designers resolve by rewriting the grammar or by stating an extra rule. But some context-free languages are inherently ambiguous, meaning that every grammar for them is ambiguous. Worse, there is no algorithm that can decide, given an arbitrary context-free grammar, whether it is ambiguous. This is one of the first undecidability results many students meet, and it explains why parser generators often report conflicts rather than silently resolving them.

Grammars generate languages; what kind of machine recognizes them? The answer is the pushdown automaton. A pushdown automaton is a nondeterministic finite automaton equipped with a stack. The stack is an unbounded memory with a strict discipline: the machine may push a symbol onto the top, pop the top symbol off, or look at the top symbol, but it cannot see or touch anything beneath the top. Each transition depends on the current state, the current input symbol or an empty move, and the symbol on top of the stack. The transition names a next state and says what to push in place of the popped symbol, which may be nothing, one symbol, or several. The machine accepts if, after reading the whole input, it is in an accept state; an alternative definition accepts when the stack is empty, and the two definitions give the same class of languages.

The stack is exactly the memory needed for matching and nesting. To recognize a to the n followed by b to the n, the machine pushes a marker for each a it reads. When it sees the first b, it switches to a second state and pops one marker for each b. If the input ends exactly when the stack returns to its initial contents, the machine accepts. If the input ends while markers remain, or if a b arrives when no markers remain, the machine rejects. The stack has given the machine an unbounded counter, and the stack discipline, which only allows access to the top, is just enough to support one such count at a time.

The fundamental theorem here is that a language is generated by a context-free grammar if and only if it is recognized by some pushdown automaton. These languages are called the context-free languages. One direction of the proof turns a grammar into a machine that simulates a leftmost derivation on its stack. The machine starts by pushing the start variable. Whenever a variable is on top of the stack, the machine nondeterministically chooses a rule for that variable, pops the variable, and pushes the right side of the rule with its leftmost symbol on top. Whenever a terminal is on top of the stack, the machine reads the next input symbol, checks that it matches, and pops it. If the input is exhausted exactly when the stack is empty, the machine accepts. Nondeterminism does the work of guessing which rule to apply, and the stack holds the part of the derivation that has not yet been matched against the input.

Just as finite automata have a pumping lemma, so do context-free languages, and it is the main tool for proving that a language is not context-free. The pumping lemma for context-free languages says that if a language is context-free, then there is a pumping length with the following property: every string in the language of at least that length can be split into five pieces, call them u, v, w, x, and y, such that v and x are not both empty, the combined length of v, w, and x is at most the pumping length, and for every nonnegative integer i, the string formed by u, then i copies of v, then w, then i copies of x, then y is in the language. The difference from the regular case is that two pieces are pumped together, in step with each other.

The proof uses parse trees rather than states. A grammar has finitely many variables, and each rule has a bounded right side, so a parse tree for a sufficiently long string must be tall. A tall enough tree has a path from the root to a leaf on which some variable appears twice. The portion of the tree between the upper occurrence and the lower occurrence of that variable generates a middle section of the string, and because the variable is the same, that portion can be removed or repeated. Removing it gives i equal to zero; repeating it gives i equal to two or more. The pieces v and x are the parts of the string generated to the left and right of the lower occurrence, and they are pumped together because they come from the same repeated subtree.

The classic non-context-free language is a to the n followed by b to the n followed by c to the n. Choose the string with pumping length many a's, then as many b's, then as many c's. The pumped section v, w, x has limited length, so it can touch at most two of the three blocks of letters. Pumping it then changes the count of at most two letters, leaving the third unchanged, and the resulting string no longer has equal counts. So the language is not context-free. This result has a concrete meaning: one stack can match two groups against each other, but not three. A related non-context-free language is the set of strings of the form w followed by w, a string repeated twice, which is why a context-free grammar cannot express the rule that a variable must be declared before it is used with the same name. Compilers handle that rule in a later phase, outside the grammar.

We have now seen two classes of languages and two kinds of machine, and we have seen that the second strictly contains the first. The language a to the n followed by b to the n separates them. The Chomsky hierarchy, named after the linguist Noam Chomsky, who introduced it in the nineteen fifties while studying the structure of natural language, extends this picture to four levels. At the bottom, called type three, are the regular languages, generated by grammars whose rules have at most one variable on the right side, placed at the end. Next, called type two, are the context-free languages, generated by grammars whose rules have a single variable on the left side. Above them, called type one, are the context-sensitive languages, generated by grammars whose rules may have context on the left side but never shorten the string. At the top, called type zero, are the recursively enumerable languages, generated by unrestricted grammars, in which any string of symbols may be rewritten as any other.

Each level has a matching machine. Regular languages correspond to finite automata. Context-free languages correspond to pushdown automata. Context-sensitive languages correspond to linear bounded automata, which are Turing machines whose tape is limited to the length of the input. Recursively enumerable languages correspond to unrestricted Turing machines, the model that defines what computation means at all. Each level strictly contains the one below it: a to the n followed by b to the n is context-free but not regular, a to the n followed by b to the n followed by c to the n is context-sensitive but not context-free, and the languages at the top include ones that no bounded machine can recognize. The hierarchy is a map of computational power, with memory as the axis: no memory, one stack, bounded tape, unbounded tape.

These ideas are not merely theoretical. They are the foundation of how programming languages are implemented. A compiler begins with a lexer, also called a scanner or tokenizer, whose job is to break the raw sequence of characters into tokens: identifiers, keywords, numbers, operators, and punctuation. Each kind of token has a simple shape. An identifier is a letter followed by any number of letters or digits. A number is a sequence of digits, possibly with a decimal point. A keyword is a fixed string. All of these shapes are regular languages, and the lexer is nothing more than a deterministic finite automaton that recognizes all of them at once. Tools that generate lexers take a list of regular expressions, one per token kind, build a nondeterministic automaton for their union using the constructions we described, convert it to a deterministic automaton with the subset construction, and then minimize the result. The output is a table-driven machine that reads each character exactly once and runs in linear time with constant memory.

The lexer hands its stream of tokens to the parser, whose job is to discover the structure of the program according to the grammar of the language. The grammar is context-free, written down explicitly in the language specification, and the parser is a pushdown automaton for it. Because general context-free parsing is too slow for large programs, language designers restrict their grammars to deterministic subclasses for which fast parsers exist. Top-down parsers, which build the parse tree from the root downward, correspond to grammars in which the next rule can be chosen by looking at a bounded number of upcoming tokens. Bottom-up parsers, which build the tree from the leaves upward and are generated by widely used parser generator tools, handle a larger class of deterministic grammars. In both cases the parser's stack is the pushdown automaton's stack, and conflicts reported by the tool are exactly points where the grammar fails to be deterministic in the required sense, often because of ambiguity.

The output of the parser is the parse tree, or more commonly a simplified form of it called an abstract syntax tree, which drops the nodes that exist only to enforce precedence and keeps the nodes that carry meaning. Later phases of the compiler walk this tree to check types, to verify that names are declared before use, to compute constant expressions, to allocate registers, and to emit machine code. The rules enforced in these phases are precisely the ones that cannot be expressed in a context-free grammar, such as the matching of a declaration with later uses of the same name, which we saw is beyond the reach of a single stack. The division of a compiler into lexer, parser, and semantic analysis is a direct reflection of the Chomsky hierarchy: regular shapes first, then context-free structure, then everything the formal models cannot express, handled by ordinary code.

A finite automaton is a machine with no memory beyond its state. A pushdown automaton is a machine with one stack. The next step, which the following section takes, is a machine with a tape it can read and write freely: the Turing machine. With that step, the hierarchy reaches its top, and the questions change from what can be recognized efficiently to what can be computed at all. The habits built here, of defining languages precisely, of proving equivalences between models, and of using pumping arguments to establish impossibility, are exactly the habits that will carry the reader through that more difficult territory.


Computability theory asks a question that sounds simple but turns out to be deep: what can be computed at all? Before we ask how fast a program can solve a problem, we must ask whether any program can solve it. This section introduces the standard model of computation, the Turing machine, and uses it to prove that some problems are beyond the reach of every program that could ever be written. The ideas here were developed in the nineteen thirties, before any electronic computer existed, yet they describe the limits of every computer built since. We will see that the limits are not about speed or memory; they are about the very notion of a step by step procedure.

The Turing machine is a mathematical model of a person working with pencil and paper under strict rules. Imagine a tape divided into cells, stretching without end to the right. Each cell holds one symbol drawn from a finite alphabet, or it holds a special blank symbol. A head sits over one cell at a time; it can read the symbol in that cell, write a new symbol in that cell, and then move one cell to the left or one cell to the right. The machine also has a finite set of internal states, and at every moment it is in exactly one state. One state is marked as the start state, and some states are marked as halting states; a halting state may be an accept state or a reject state.

The behavior of the machine is fixed by a transition function. The transition function is a finite table of rules. Each rule says: if the machine is in this state and the head reads this symbol, then write that symbol, move the head in that direction, and enter that state. Because the set of states is finite and the alphabet is finite, the transition function is a finite object; it can be written down on a single page. A computation begins with the input written on the tape, the head over the leftmost cell, and the machine in the start state. The machine then follows the rules one step at a time. If it ever enters a halting state, the computation is over and the output is whatever remains on the tape, or simply the fact of acceptance or rejection. If it never enters a halting state, the machine runs forever.

It may seem that such a crude device could not capture the richness of real computation. The tape is only one dimensional, the head moves one cell at a time, and the machine can remember only a finite amount of information in its state. Yet the tape has no bound on its length, so the machine has unbounded scratch space, and the finite control is enough to implement loops, counters, and subroutines. With patience one can build a Turing machine that adds numbers, multiplies numbers, sorts a list, searches a tree, or simulates any circuit. Every feature of a modern programming language can be translated into a sequence of tape operations. The translation is tedious but entirely mechanical, which is exactly the point: the Turing machine strips computation down to its smallest moving parts.

Why should we believe that the Turing machine captures the idea of computation itself? The argument given by Turing was an analysis of what a human computer does when following an algorithm. The person looks at a small number of symbols at a time, is in one of a finite number of mental states, writes a symbol, and shifts attention to nearby symbols. Any procedure that a person can carry out by rote can be decomposed into such steps. Mathematicians proposed several other models at about the same time, including the lambda calculus and systems of recursive functions, and each was motivated by a different intuition. Remarkably, all of them turned out to compute exactly the same class of functions. Any function computable by one model is computable by every other model.

This convergence is the evidence behind the Church-Turing thesis. The thesis states that every function that can be computed by any effective procedure can be computed by a Turing machine. It is called a thesis rather than a theorem because one side of the claim, the informal notion of an effective procedure, is not a mathematical object and cannot be the subject of a proof. The thesis is a statement that our formal definition matches our informal intuition. It has survived nearly a century of attempts to find a counterexample. Every new programming language, every new architecture, every model of parallel or quantum computation that has been proposed can be simulated by a Turing machine, perhaps slowly, but exactly. For the purposes of this section we accept the thesis, and we use the word program as a synonym for Turing machine.

A central consequence of the finite nature of the transition function is that a Turing machine can itself be written down as a string of symbols. We can agree on a scheme that encodes the number of states, the alphabet, and every rule in the table as a single string over a fixed alphabet. Once this is done, a machine is simply data. It can be written on a tape and read by another machine. This observation might seem trivial, but it is the seed of the stored program computer and of nearly every result in the rest of this section.

Turing showed that there exists a single machine, the universal Turing machine, which takes as input the description of any other machine together with an input for that machine, and simulates that machine on that input step by step. The universal machine reads the encoded transition table, keeps track of the simulated state and the simulated head position on its own tape, and performs each simulated step by looking up the appropriate rule. If the simulated machine halts, the universal machine halts with the same answer. If the simulated machine runs forever, the universal machine runs forever too. The universal machine is the theoretical ancestor of every general purpose computer: a fixed piece of hardware that becomes any machine at all once the right program is loaded into it.

The existence of the universal machine means we never need to build special hardware for each new problem. We write a program, we feed it as data to the universal machine, and the universal machine behaves as though it were that program. Interpreters, compilers, and virtual machines are all practical versions of this one idea. It also means that programs can be examined, transformed, and run by other programs. A program that reads another program as input is not exotic; it is the normal situation in computer science. Keep this in mind, because the hardest results below are all about what happens when a program is asked questions about other programs.

To speak precisely about problems, we use the language of formal languages. An alphabet is a finite set of symbols, and a string is a finite sequence of symbols from the alphabet. A language is any set of strings. Every yes or no question about strings can be identified with the language of strings for which the answer is yes. For example, the question of whether a binary string represents a prime number corresponds to the language of all binary strings that represent primes. A decision problem is just a language, and solving the problem means telling, for any given string, whether it belongs to the language.

A Turing machine decides a language if, for every input string, the machine halts and accepts when the string is in the language and halts and rejects when the string is not in the language. The key word is halts: a decider always stops, no matter what input it is given. A language that has a decider is called decidable, or sometimes recursive. Decidable languages correspond to problems that a program can solve completely and reliably. The set of primes is decidable, the set of balanced parentheses is decidable, and the set of valid chess positions is decidable.

A weaker notion is recognition. A Turing machine recognizes a language if, for every string in the language, the machine halts and accepts, and for every string not in the language, the machine either halts and rejects or runs forever. A recognizer is allowed to loop on inputs outside the language. A language that has a recognizer is called recognizable, or recursively enumerable. Every decidable language is recognizable, because a decider is a recognizer that happens never to loop. The converse is false, and showing this is one of the main goals of this section. The gap between deciding and recognizing is the gap between an answer you will always receive and an answer that may never arrive.

There is a useful symmetry here. If a language and its complement are both recognizable, then the language is decidable. To see this, run the recognizer for the language and the recognizer for the complement at the same time, alternating one step of each. Every string is either in the language or in the complement, so one of the two recognizers must eventually accept. When it does, we know the answer and can halt. This means that a recognizable language whose complement is not recognizable can never be decidable. We will use this observation when we meet the halting problem.

The halting problem is the following question. Given the description of a Turing machine and an input string for that machine, does the machine halt on that input? This is a well defined yes or no question about a pair of strings, so it corresponds to a language: the set of all pairs consisting of a machine description and an input such that the machine halts on the input. The halting problem is recognizable, because the universal machine can simulate the given machine on the given input and accept if the simulation ever halts. If the simulation never halts, the universal machine never answers, which is allowed for a recognizer. The question is whether we can do better and decide the halting problem outright.

We now prove that the halting problem is not decidable. The proof uses a technique called diagonalization, and it proceeds by contradiction. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that there is a Turing machine, call it the halting decider, that solves the halting problem. The halting decider takes a machine description and an input, always halts, and correctly answers yes if the described machine would halt on that input and no otherwise. We will use this supposed decider to build a second machine, and then we will show that the second machine behaves impossibly.

The second machine, which we may call the contrary machine, takes as its input the description of some machine. It then does something slightly strange: it asks the halting decider whether that machine halts when given its own description as input. In other words, it asks whether the input machine halts when it is run on itself. There is nothing illegitimate about this, because a machine description is just a string, and a string can be fed to any machine, including the machine it describes. If the halting decider answers that the input machine would halt on itself, the contrary machine deliberately enters an infinite loop and never halts. If the halting decider answers that the input machine would not halt on itself, the contrary machine immediately halts.

The contrary machine does the opposite of whatever the input machine does on itself. Now we ask the decisive question: what happens when the contrary machine is given its own description as input? Suppose the contrary machine halts on its own description. Then, by its construction, the halting decider must have reported that the contrary machine does not halt on its own description; but the decider is assumed correct, so the contrary machine does not halt. That contradicts our supposition. So suppose instead that the contrary machine does not halt on its own description. Then the halting decider must have reported that it does halt, and since the decider is correct, the contrary machine halts. Again a contradiction.

Both cases lead to a contradiction, so our original assumption must be wrong: no halting decider exists. The halting problem is undecidable. Notice how little the proof used. It did not depend on any detail of the tape or the states. It used only three facts: that machines can be described as strings, that a machine can be built which calls another machine as a subroutine, and that a machine can be run on its own description. Any model of computation with these three properties, which is to say any reasonable programming language, has its own undecidable halting problem.

The name diagonalization comes from a picture. Imagine a table with one row for every machine and one column for every machine description, where the entry in a row and column records whether the row machine halts on the column description. The contrary machine is built by walking down the diagonal of this table and flipping every entry. The resulting row of answers differs from every row in the table at its diagonal position, so it cannot be any row of the table. Yet if the halting decider existed, the contrary machine would be a legitimate machine and would have to appear as some row. The same argument, in a different costume, was used by Cantor to show that the real numbers cannot be listed, and we will return to that connection shortly.

Having one undecidable problem in hand, we can show that many other problems are undecidable without repeating the diagonal argument each time. The tool for this is reduction. A reduction from problem A to problem B is a computable transformation that turns any instance of A into an instance of B in such a way that the answer is preserved: the original instance is a yes instance of A exactly when the transformed instance is a yes instance of B. If such a reduction exists, then a decider for B would give a decider for A, because we could transform the input and then run the decider for B. Turning this around, if A is known to be undecidable, then B must be undecidable too, since otherwise we could decide A.

The direction of a reduction is easy to get backwards, so it is worth stating carefully. To prove that a new problem is undecidable, we reduce a known undecidable problem to the new problem. We are showing that the new problem is at least as hard as the old one. The halting problem serves as the known undecidable problem in most such proofs. A typical argument goes like this: suppose there were a decider for the new problem; then here is how we would use it to decide halting; but halting is undecidable; therefore the supposed decider does not exist.

Consider the problem of deciding whether a given Turing machine accepts a given input. This is called the acceptance problem. We reduce the halting problem to it. Given a machine and an input, modify the machine so that every reject state is changed into an accept state. The modified machine accepts the input exactly when the original machine halts on the input, because halting now always means accepting. The modification is a simple edit of the transition table, so it is computable. A decider for acceptance would therefore give a decider for halting, which is impossible. So the acceptance problem is undecidable.

Consider next the problem of deciding whether a given machine halts on the empty input, with nothing written on the tape. We reduce the general halting problem to this one. Given a machine and an input string, build a new machine that first writes the input string onto its tape, ignoring whatever was there, and then behaves exactly like the original machine. The new machine halts on the empty input exactly when the original machine halts on the given input. Again the construction is a mechanical edit of the program, so the empty input halting problem is undecidable. This small trick, of hardwiring an input into a program, is used over and over in the subject.

A third example is the problem of deciding whether a given machine halts on at least one input, or on every input, or on infinitely many inputs. Each of these can be shown undecidable by a reduction of the same flavor. Given a machine and an input, build a new machine that ignores its own input, writes the fixed input on the tape, and simulates the original machine. The new machine halts on every input if the original halts on the fixed input, and halts on no input otherwise. So any question that distinguishes the machine that halts everywhere from the machine that halts nowhere is undecidable. Once this pattern is understood, a general principle suggests itself.

That principle is Rice's theorem, which can be stated in words as follows. Any nontrivial property of the language recognized by a Turing machine is undecidable. A property of the language is a property that depends only on which strings the machine accepts, not on how the machine is built or how many steps it takes. Nontrivial means that some machines have the property and some do not. Examples of such properties include: the machine accepts the empty string; the machine accepts every string; the machine accepts only strings of even length; the machine accepts a finite language; the machine accepts the same language as some other fixed machine. Rice's theorem says that every single one of these is undecidable.

The proof of Rice's theorem is a reduction from the halting problem, generalizing the trick above. Suppose the property in question is not held by the machine that accepts nothing; if it is, we work with the complementary property instead. Since the property is nontrivial, there is some machine that does have it; call it the witness machine. Now, given any machine and input for the halting problem, construct a new machine that first simulates the given machine on the given input, and if that simulation halts, proceeds to behave like the witness machine on the original input. If the given machine halts on the given input, the new machine accepts exactly what the witness machine accepts, so it has the property. If the given machine never halts, the new machine never gets past the simulation and accepts nothing, so it lacks the property. A decider for the property would thus decide halting.

Rice's theorem is sweeping. It tells us that we cannot write a program that reliably inspects arbitrary programs and determines anything interesting about their input output behavior. We cannot build a perfect virus detector that decides whether a program will ever perform a malicious action. We cannot build a perfect checker that decides whether a program ever outputs a particular string. We cannot decide whether two programs compute the same function, which is the problem of program equivalence. Each of these is a nontrivial property of the language or function computed, and so each is undecidable. This does not mean that useful approximations are impossible; it means that any such tool must sometimes answer incorrectly, sometimes refuse to answer, or restrict itself to a narrow class of programs.

It is important to be clear about what Rice's theorem does not say. Properties of the program text itself, rather than of its behavior, are often decidable. Whether a program has more than one hundred states is decidable; just count. Whether a program ever moves its head left is decidable; just check the table. Whether a program halts within one thousand steps on a given input is decidable; just simulate for one thousand steps. The theorem concerns semantic properties, those that ask about the function the program computes, not syntactic properties that ask about the shape of the program. The line between syntax and semantics is exactly the line between what a compiler can check and what it cannot.

Program equivalence deserves a closer look because it arises so often in practice. The problem asks: given two programs, do they produce the same output on every input? A decider for this problem would be enormously valuable. It would let us verify that an optimized program matches its unoptimized version, that a rewritten module behaves like the module it replaced, or that a student's submission matches a reference solution. The reduction from halting is short. Given a machine and an input, build a program that halts and accepts if the machine halts on the input, and runs forever otherwise. Compare this program to a program that accepts nothing. The two are equivalent exactly when the machine does not halt on the input. So equivalence is undecidable, and in fact it is not even recognizable, because its complement, nonequivalence, is only recognizable in a weak sense that does not combine to give a decider.

Not every undecidable problem looks like a question about programs. The Post correspondence problem is a puzzle about strings that has nothing to do with machines on its surface. You are given a finite collection of dominoes. Each domino has a top string and a bottom string, both over the same alphabet. The question is whether you can choose a sequence of dominoes, repeating dominoes as often as you like, so that the string formed by reading all the top halves in order is exactly the same as the string formed by reading all the bottom halves in order. For a particular collection one can often find a match by trial and error or convince oneself that no match exists. The question is whether there is a uniform procedure that works for every collection.

There is not. The Post correspondence problem is undecidable. The proof is a reduction from the halting problem in which the dominoes are designed so that any match must spell out a complete, valid, halting computation history of a given machine on a given input. The top strings lag one configuration behind the bottom strings, and the dominoes encode the transition rules so that each configuration must be followed by the correct next configuration. A match exists exactly when the machine halts. The construction is intricate, but the lesson is simple: undecidability is not confined to self referential questions about programs. It can hide inside combinatorial puzzles, inside questions about matrices, inside questions about equations with integer solutions, and inside questions about the tilings of the plane.

We now turn to a different way of seeing why undecidable problems must exist, one that does not require constructing any particular problem. The argument is a counting argument, and it needs a few ideas about the sizes of infinite sets. Two sets have the same size if their elements can be paired off, one to one, with nothing left over on either side. A set is countable if it is finite or if its elements can be paired off with the natural numbers one, two, three, and so on. Equivalently, a set is countable if its elements can be arranged in an infinite list, so that every element appears at some finite position.

The set of all finite strings over a fixed alphabet is countable. To list them, first write the empty string, then all strings of length one in alphabetical order, then all strings of length two in alphabetical order, and so on. Every string has some finite length and so appears at some finite position in the list. Since every Turing machine can be described by a finite string, the set of all Turing machines is countable. We can list every program that could ever be written: program number one, program number two, and so on without end. The list is infinite, but it is only countably infinite.

Now consider the set of all languages over the alphabet, that is, the set of all sets of strings. We claim this set is uncountable: it cannot be arranged in a list. The proof is again diagonalization. Suppose someone hands us a proposed list of all languages: language one, language two, language three, and so on. We will build a language that is not on the list. Take the list of all strings, string one, string two, string three, from the previous paragraph. Define a new language by the rule: string number k belongs to the new language exactly when string number k does not belong to language number k. The new language differs from language one at string one, from language two at string two, and in general from language number k at string number k. So it differs from every language on the list and is not on the list. No list can contain all languages, so the set of languages is uncountable.

Put the two counts side by side. There are countably many programs. There are uncountably many languages, which is to say uncountably many decision problems. Each program decides at most one language. So the programs can cover only a countable collection of languages, and the overwhelming majority of languages have no program that decides them. In a precise sense, almost every problem is undecidable. The decidable problems are a vanishingly small island in an ocean of problems that no program can solve. The halting problem is remarkable not because it is undecidable but because it is a natural, simply stated problem that happens to lie in the ocean.

This counting argument also clarifies the relationship between the diagonal proof for halting and the diagonal proof for uncountability. They are the same argument. In both cases we have a list, we walk down the diagonal, and we construct something that disagrees with every entry at its own position. For languages, the construction produces a language not on the list. For programs, the construction would produce a program not on the list, which is impossible since the list contains all programs; so the construction must fail at some step, and the only step that can fail is the assumed halting decider. The halting proof is the uncountability proof with the extra constraint that the diagonal object must itself be a program.

We should pause on the phrase every program that could ever be written. The limits described here do not depend on the current state of technology. They do not depend on the speed of the processor, the amount of memory, the number of cores, or the cleverness of the programmer. A machine a million times faster than any existing computer would face exactly the same halting problem, because the proof never mentioned speed. A machine with more memory than there are atoms in the universe would also face it, because the proof granted the Turing machine an unbounded tape from the start. The limits are consequences of the definition of computation, not consequences of engineering.

Some have asked whether a different kind of machine might escape. Could a machine that consults an oracle, a black box that answers the halting problem instantly, decide everything? Turing considered exactly this. A machine with a halting oracle can decide the halting problem, of course, but it has its own halting problem: whether a given oracle machine halts on a given input. The diagonal argument applies again, word for word, and this new problem is undecidable even with the oracle. Adding a second oracle produces a third undecidable problem, and so on through an infinite hierarchy of degrees of unsolvability. There is no top. Every level of computational power has problems it cannot solve.

The philosophical consequences of these results are considerable, and we should be careful to state them neither too weakly nor too strongly. The weak statement is that there are specific questions, such as whether a given program halts, that no program can answer in general. The strong and incorrect statement is that computers are feeble or that human minds obviously surpass them. Nothing in computability theory shows that human beings can decide the halting problem. A person who stares at a program and announces that it halts is making an informed guess, and for sufficiently complicated programs the guess will sometimes be wrong. The limits apply to any system that follows rules, and it is an open question whether minds are such systems.

A more useful consequence concerns the design of software tools. Every compiler, type checker, static analyzer, and verification tool is attempting to answer questions about the behavior of programs. Rice's theorem guarantees that for any interesting question, a tool that always answers correctly and always halts cannot exist. Tool designers therefore make a trade. They may design the tool to be sound but incomplete, so that it never claims a bad program is good but sometimes rejects a good program. They may design it to be complete but unsound, catching every bad program at the cost of occasional false alarms. Or they may restrict the language of programs so severely that the question becomes decidable, which is the approach taken by type systems that forbid unbounded recursion. Each trade is a direct response to undecidability.

The results also reshape how we think about mathematical truth. Hilbert had hoped for a decision procedure that would settle any mathematical statement, a mechanical method that would take a statement as input and output whether it is provable. The undecidability of the halting problem destroys this hope. For any sufficiently powerful system of axioms, the question of whether a given statement is provable is undecidable, because one can encode the statement that a particular machine halts and ask whether it is provable. Since halting is undecidable, provability is undecidable. Closely related is the incompleteness theorem of Godel, which shows that in any such system there are true statements that cannot be proved. Both results draw on the same self referential trick: a statement or program that talks about itself.

There is something beautiful in the way the limits arise from the strengths. The very feature that makes computers universal, the ability to treat programs as data and run one program on another, is the feature that makes the halting problem undecidable. If programs could not read other programs, the diagonal argument could not be carried out. A less powerful model, such as a finite automaton, has a decidable halting problem precisely because it is too weak to simulate itself. Universality and undecidability are two faces of the same coin. You cannot have a machine that can do everything computable without also having questions about that machine that it cannot answer.

Let us summarize the landscape. At the bottom are the decidable languages, those for which a program always halts with the correct answer. Above them are the recognizable languages, those for which a program halts with yes on every member but may loop forever on nonmembers. The halting problem lives in the second class but not the first. Its complement, the set of machine and input pairs where the machine runs forever, is not even recognizable, because if it were, we could combine the two recognizers into a decider. Above the recognizable languages lie uncountably many languages that are neither recognizable nor co-recognizable, most of which no one will ever name. Reductions move problems around within this landscape and let us place new problems by comparing them to old ones.

Computability theory is often presented as a collection of negative results, and in a narrow sense that is accurate: its most famous theorems say that something cannot be done. Yet the mood of the subject is not pessimistic. The impossibility proofs are also proofs of the universality of computation, of the equivalence of all reasonable models, and of the astonishing fact that a single finite table of rules can simulate any procedure whatsoever. They draw a line around what computation is, and in drawing it they make the territory inside the line precise. Every program that has ever run, and every program that ever will, lives inside that line. The next section will begin to map the territory inside by asking not whether problems can be solved but how expensive solving them must be.


The previous sections of this book asked which problems a machine can solve at all. This section asks a sharper question: which problems can a machine solve with a reasonable amount of time and memory? The study of this question is called computational complexity theory, and it is the subject of the present section. Computability theory draws a line between the solvable and the unsolvable. Complexity theory draws lines inside the solvable region, separating practical problems from problems that are solvable in principle but hopeless in practice. These lines turn out to be surprisingly hard to draw with certainty, and the central question of the field remains open after more than fifty years of effort.

To talk about complexity, we first need a precise notion of a resource. The two resources we care about most are time and space. Time is the number of steps a machine takes before it halts, and space is the number of tape cells (or memory cells) it writes on during the computation. Both are measured as functions of the size of the input, which we write in words as n. An algorithm that takes at most n squared steps on every input of size n is said to run in time n squared. An algorithm that uses at most n cells of memory is said to run in space n. We always measure the worst case over all inputs of a given size, because we want a guarantee that holds no matter which instance we are handed. We also ignore constant factors and lower order terms, because the exact constants depend on details of the machine model that have nothing to do with the problem itself.

Ignoring constants may seem careless, but it is what allows the theory to be robust. A Turing machine with one tape can simulate a Turing machine with many tapes with only a polynomial slowdown. A random access machine of the kind that resembles a real computer can be simulated by a Turing machine with only a polynomial slowdown. In every reasonable model of computation that has been proposed, the running time changes by at most a polynomial factor when we switch from one model to another. This observation is sometimes called the extended Church-Turing thesis, and it is the reason the theory focuses on the distinction between polynomial time and more than polynomial time rather than on finer distinctions.

This brings us to the class P, which is built on decision problems. A decision problem is a problem whose answer on every instance is either yes or no. The class P is the set of all decision problems that a deterministic Turing machine can solve in polynomial time. Polynomial time means time at most n to the power of k for some fixed constant k. The constant k may be one, or two, or a hundred, but it must not grow with the input. Many familiar problems belong to P, and a few examples will make the class concrete. Sorting a list is in P, deciding whether a graph is connected is in P, and finding a shortest path between two vertices is in P. Deciding whether a number is prime was shown to be in P in 2002, a result that surprised many people who had assumed the problem might lie just outside.

Why is polynomial time the right dividing line between easy and hard? There are several reasons, and none of them is airtight, but together they are convincing. The first reason is that P is closed under composition. If you run a polynomial time algorithm and then feed its output to another polynomial time algorithm, the combined procedure still runs in polynomial time, because the composition of two polynomials is a polynomial. If you call a polynomial time subroutine a polynomial number of times, the result is still polynomial time. This means that P is closed under the ordinary ways that programmers build large algorithms from small ones, and that is exactly the property we want from a class of feasible problems.

The second reason is the invariance just discussed. The class P is the same whether we define it using one tape Turing machines, many tape Turing machines, random access machines, or any other standard model. A class of problems that depended on the fine details of the machine would be a statement about the machine, not about the problems. The third reason is empirical, and it rests on decades of experience. In practice, when a problem is found to be in P, the exponent in the polynomial is almost always small, typically one, two, or three, and the algorithm is actually usable. When a problem is not known to be in P, the best known algorithms almost always take exponential time, meaning time like two to the power of n. Such algorithms become useless when n is more than a few dozen. The gap between n cubed and two to the power of n is not a gap of degree; it is a gap of kind.

We now turn to the class NP, which is the most important class in the whole theory. There are two equivalent ways to define it, and both are worth understanding. The first definition uses the idea of a verifier. Consider a decision problem, and suppose that for every yes instance there exists a short piece of evidence, called a certificate or a witness, that convinces us the answer is yes. Suppose further that there is a polynomial time algorithm, called the verifier, which takes an instance and a proposed certificate and checks whether the certificate is valid. If such a verifier exists, with certificates of polynomial length, then the problem belongs to NP. The letters NP stand for nondeterministic polynomial time, for reasons that the second definition will make clear. It is often helpful to think of NP as the class of problems whose yes answers can be verified in polynomial time.

The key point is that NP does not require us to find the certificate quickly. It only requires that a certificate, once found, can be checked quickly. This is the difference between solving a puzzle and checking a proposed solution to the puzzle. Checking is usually easy; solving may or may not be. Every problem in P is also in NP, because a problem in P can be verified by simply ignoring the certificate and solving the problem directly. So P is contained in NP, and this containment is the starting point of everything that follows. The great open question, which we will come to shortly, is whether the containment is strict.

The second definition of NP uses nondeterminism. A nondeterministic Turing machine is a Turing machine that, at each step, may have several possible moves rather than exactly one. We say that such a machine accepts an input if there exists at least one sequence of choices that leads to an accepting state. The machine is not a realistic model of a physical device; rather, it is a mathematical device for capturing the idea of guessing. A nondeterministic machine running in polynomial time can guess a certificate, one symbol at a time, and then verify it. Conversely, if a nondeterministic machine accepts an input, the sequence of choices it made is itself a certificate that a deterministic verifier can check. So the class of problems accepted by nondeterministic polynomial time machines is exactly the class of problems with polynomial time verifiers, and both are NP.

Let us now look at some concrete problems in NP, since the class is best understood through examples. The first and most famous is the satisfiability problem, usually called SAT. An instance of SAT is a Boolean formula built from variables, the operations and, or, and not, and parentheses. The question is whether there exists an assignment of true and false to the variables that makes the whole formula true. The certificate is the assignment itself, and the verifier simply plugs the values in and evaluates the formula, which takes polynomial time. Finding a satisfying assignment, on the other hand, seems to require trying many possibilities, and with n variables there are two to the power of n possible assignments. Satisfiability is in NP, and no polynomial time algorithm for it is known.

The second example is the Hamiltonian path problem. An instance is a graph, and the question is whether there is a path that visits every vertex exactly once. The certificate is the list of vertices in the order the path visits them, and the verifier checks that consecutive vertices are joined by an edge and that every vertex appears exactly once. This check takes polynomial time in the number of vertices. Finding such a path is another matter; a graph with n vertices has n factorial possible orderings, and no known algorithm does substantially better than examining a large fraction of them in the worst case.

The third example is subset sum. An instance is a list of integers together with a target integer, and the question is whether some subset of the list adds up exactly to the target. The certificate is the subset, and the verifier adds up its elements and compares the sum to the target. Notice that the integers may be large, so the input size is measured in the number of digits rather than in the magnitude of the numbers. An algorithm that runs in time proportional to the target value is not polynomial in the input size, because the target can be written in a small number of digits but have an enormous value. This subtlety matters, and we will return to it when we discuss pseudo-polynomial algorithms.

The fourth example is graph coloring. An instance is a graph and a number k, and the question is whether the vertices can be colored with k colors so that no two adjacent vertices receive the same color. The certificate is the coloring, and the verifier checks every edge. For k equal to two the problem is in P, because a graph is two colorable exactly when it has no odd cycle, which is easy to check. For k equal to three the problem is in NP and no polynomial time algorithm is known. The sharp jump in apparent difficulty between two colors and three colors is typical of the field.

The fifth example is vertex cover. An instance is a graph and a number k, and the question is whether there is a set of at most k vertices such that every edge touches at least one vertex in the set. The certificate is the set, and the verifier checks each edge. The sixth example is the traveling salesman problem in its decision form. An instance is a set of cities with distances between them and a budget. The question is whether there is a tour that visits every city exactly once, returns to the start, and has total length at most the budget. The certificate is the tour, and the verifier adds up the distances. All six of these problems are in NP, and for all six, decades of effort have failed to produce a polynomial time algorithm.

These problems look very different on the surface. One is about logic, one is about paths in graphs, one is about sums of numbers, one is about colors, one is about covering edges, and one is about tours. The remarkable discovery of the early nineteen seventies is that, from the point of view of complexity theory, they are all the same problem in disguise. The tool that makes this discovery precise is the polynomial time reduction, and we now define it carefully.

Let A and B be two decision problems. A polynomial time reduction from A to B is a polynomial time algorithm that transforms every instance of A into an instance of B. The transformation must preserve the answer: the A instance is a yes instance if and only if the transformed B instance is a yes instance. When such a reduction exists, we say that A reduces to B, and we say that B is at least as hard as A. The reasoning behind this claim is simple and worth stating in full. If we had a polynomial time algorithm for B, we could solve A in polynomial time by first applying the reduction and then running the algorithm for B. The reduction takes polynomial time, the B algorithm takes polynomial time on the transformed instance, and the transformed instance has polynomial size, so the whole procedure takes polynomial time. Thus if B is in P, then A is in P. Equivalently, if A is not in P, then B is not in P.

Reductions are transitive, which is a property of the first importance. If A reduces to B and B reduces to C, then A reduces to C, because we can compose the two transformations and the composition still runs in polynomial time. This transitivity is what allows us to build long chains of reductions and to organize hundreds of problems into a single structure. Reductions are also the main technical skill of the working complexity theorist. Constructing a reduction means building a gadget: a small piece of a B instance that simulates one piece of an A instance. The gadgets must fit together so that the whole B instance faithfully encodes the whole A instance.

We can now define the central concept. A problem B is NP-hard if every problem in NP reduces to B in polynomial time. A problem B is NP-complete if B is NP-hard and B is also in NP. An NP-complete problem is thus a hardest problem in NP: it is in the class, and everything in the class reduces to it. The consequence of this definition is striking. If any single NP-complete problem has a polynomial time algorithm, then every problem in NP has a polynomial time algorithm, because every problem in NP reduces to that one. In that case P would equal NP. Conversely, if P does not equal NP, then no NP-complete problem has a polynomial time algorithm. The NP-complete problems stand or fall together.

It is not at all obvious that NP-complete problems exist. The definition requires a single problem to which every problem in NP reduces, including problems nobody has thought of yet. The theorem that establishes the existence of such a problem is the Cook-Levin theorem, proved independently by Stephen Cook in 1971 and by Leonid Levin at around the same time. The theorem states that satisfiability is NP-complete. We have already seen that satisfiability is in NP, so the content of the theorem is that every problem in NP reduces to satisfiability.

The proof of the Cook-Levin theorem is worth describing in words, because the idea is beautiful and the details are mechanical. Take any problem in NP whatsoever, including one nobody has yet imagined. By definition there is a nondeterministic Turing machine that decides it in polynomial time, say in time n to the power of k. Fix an instance of size n, and consider what the machine does on it. The computation of the machine on this instance, along any sequence of choices, consists of at most n to the power of k steps. At each step the machine uses at most n to the power of k tape cells. So the entire computation can be laid out as a table with n to the power of k rows and n to the power of k columns. Each row is the complete configuration of the machine at one moment: the contents of the tape, the position of the head, and the current state. The table has polynomial size, and that is the key fact.

Now we introduce Boolean variables to describe the contents of the table. For each cell of the table and each possible symbol that could appear there, we have a variable that is true if and only if that cell holds that symbol. Further variables record the state and the head position at each step. Then we write down a Boolean formula that says four things. The first row of the table is the starting configuration on the given input. Every row after the first follows from the row before it by one legal move of the machine. Some row contains the accepting state, and every cell contains exactly one symbol. Each of these conditions can be expressed as a formula whose size is polynomial in n. The legality of a move depends only on a constant size window of the table, and there are only polynomially many windows. The conjunction of all these formulas is satisfiable if and only if there exists an accepting computation, which is exactly the condition for the instance to be a yes instance. The construction of the formula takes polynomial time. This is the reduction, and the theorem is proved.

Once satisfiability was known to be NP-complete, the floodgates opened. In 1972 Richard Karp published a paper showing that twenty one other natural problems are NP-complete, including the Hamiltonian path problem, subset sum, graph coloring, vertex cover, and the traveling salesman problem. His method was not to repeat the Cook-Levin construction for each problem, but to use transitivity. To show that a new problem B is NP-complete, it suffices to show that B is in NP and that some problem already known to be NP-complete reduces to B. Karp reduced satisfiability to three-satisfiability, a restricted version in which every clause has exactly three literals, and then reduced three-satisfiability to a handful of graph problems, and then reduced those graph problems to further problems. The resulting tree of reductions has since grown to include thousands of problems from every area of science and engineering.

The practical consequence of all this is a change in strategy. When a researcher encounters a new problem and cannot find an efficient algorithm, the first thing to try is to show that the problem is NP-complete by reducing a known NP-complete problem to it. If this succeeds, the researcher has not proved that no efficient algorithm exists, but has proved that finding one would resolve the P versus NP question, which is widely regarded as beyond reach. This is a respectable outcome, not a failure. It tells the researcher to stop looking for an exact polynomial time algorithm and to start looking for the kinds of compromises we describe later in this section.

We arrive at the P versus NP question itself. Is P equal to NP, or is it not? In words: is every problem whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time also a problem whose solutions can be found in polynomial time? Almost every expert believes the answer is no, that P is a proper subset of NP, and that the NP-complete problems have no polynomial time algorithm. But nobody has proved it, and the gap is not a small one. The question was posed clearly in 1971, it is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems with a one million dollar reward, and it has resisted every attack.

Why does the question matter so much? One reason is practical, and it concerns applied computer science. Thousands of problems in scheduling, logistics, circuit design, protein folding, and machine learning are NP-complete. If P equals NP, all of them have efficient algorithms, and much of applied computer science would be transformed overnight. Another reason is conceptual, and it concerns the nature of thought itself. The question asks whether creativity can be automated. Checking a proof is a mechanical task that a verifier can do; finding a proof seems to require insight. If P equals NP, then finding a proof of polynomial length is no harder, up to a polynomial factor, than checking it. Every theorem with a short proof could be proved by machine in reasonable time. A third reason is security, and it concerns every encrypted message sent today. Modern cryptography rests on the assumption that certain problems, such as factoring large integers, cannot be solved in polynomial time. If P equals NP, then those problems are easy, and every widely used encryption scheme is broken.

Why is the question so hard to resolve? One answer is that the natural approaches have been shown not to work. Diagonalization, the technique used to separate decidable from undecidable problems, cannot separate P from NP. There exist oracle worlds in which P equals NP and other oracle worlds in which P does not equal NP, and diagonalization arguments work the same way relative to every oracle. A second approach, based on proving that no small Boolean circuit can compute satisfiability, ran into a barrier called natural proofs. This barrier shows that the techniques used to prove circuit lower bounds for weak circuit classes cannot, under plausible cryptographic assumptions, extend to the strong classes needed. A third barrier, called algebrization, rules out a further family of techniques. Any proof that P does not equal NP must go around all three barriers, and nobody knows how.

Before moving beyond NP, we should introduce its mirror image, the class coNP. A problem is in coNP if its complement is in NP, where the complement swaps the yes and no answers. For example, the problem of deciding whether a Boolean formula is unsatisfiable is in coNP, because its complement, satisfiability, is in NP. Equivalently, a problem is in coNP if every no instance has a short certificate that a polynomial time verifier can check. For unsatisfiability, a no instance is a satisfiable formula, and the certificate is a satisfying assignment. A yes instance of unsatisfiability, by contrast, seems to have no short certificate; to convince someone that no assignment works, we seem to need to list all of them.

The relationship between NP and coNP is another open question. If NP equaled coNP, then every unsatisfiable formula would have a short proof of its unsatisfiability, which would be a remarkable fact about logic. Most researchers believe that NP does not equal coNP, which would imply that P does not equal NP, since P is closed under complement and thus P equals coP. The intersection of NP and coNP is also interesting. Problems in the intersection have short certificates for both yes and no answers. Integer factoring, in its decision version, lies in this intersection, and so does linear programming, which was later shown to be in P. The fact that factoring lies in the intersection is one reason many believe factoring is not NP-complete, since if it were, NP would equal coNP.

So far we have discussed only time. Space is the other fundamental resource, and the class PSPACE is the set of decision problems that can be solved by a deterministic machine using a polynomial amount of memory, with no limit on the time. Since a machine that runs for polynomial time can touch only polynomially many cells, P is contained in PSPACE. More surprisingly, NP is also contained in PSPACE, because a polynomial space machine can simply try every possible certificate one after another, reusing the same memory for each attempt. The time taken is exponential, but the space is polynomial. By the same reasoning coNP is contained in PSPACE. A famous theorem of Savitch shows that nondeterministic polynomial space equals deterministic polynomial space, so, unlike the situation for time, nondeterminism does not seem to help when space is the resource being measured.

The canonical PSPACE-complete problem is the quantified Boolean formula problem. Here a Boolean formula is preceded by a string of alternating quantifiers: there exists a value for one variable, for all values of the next variable, there exists a value for the next, and so on. The question is whether the quantified statement is true. Satisfiability is the special case with only existential quantifiers. The alternation of quantifiers captures the structure of a two player game. One player chooses a move, the opponent responds, the first player responds again, and the question is whether the first player has a winning strategy. Whether P equals PSPACE is open, and so is whether NP equals PSPACE, though both are believed to be false.

Between NP and PSPACE lies a structure called the polynomial hierarchy. The idea is to count alternations of quantifiers. NP corresponds to problems with a single existential quantifier followed by a polynomial time check. The next level up consists of problems of the form, there exists a certificate such that for all possible responses the check succeeds, which is an existential quantifier followed by a universal one. The level after that adds another alternation, and so on. The polynomial hierarchy is the union of all these levels. If P equals NP, the whole hierarchy collapses to P. If NP equals coNP, the whole hierarchy collapses to NP. It is widely believed that the hierarchy is infinite, with each level strictly larger than the one below. This belief is used as a working hypothesis in many results that say, if such and such were true, the polynomial hierarchy would collapse, and therefore such and such is probably false.

Above PSPACE lies EXPTIME, the class of problems solvable in deterministic time two to the power of a polynomial in n. Every problem in PSPACE is in EXPTIME, because a polynomial space machine has only exponentially many configurations and must halt within that many steps or loop forever. Generalized chess and checkers on a board of size n are EXPTIME-complete.

Although almost all the separations between these classes are open, one separation is known with certainty, and it comes from the time hierarchy theorem. The theorem says, roughly, that if one time bound is sufficiently larger than another, then there are problems solvable within the larger bound that are not solvable within the smaller one. More time really does buy more computational power. The proof is by diagonalization, in the style of the halting problem argument from earlier in this book. We construct a machine that, on input a description of a machine M, simulates M on its own description for the larger time bound and then does the opposite of what M does. This machine runs within the larger bound but cannot run within the smaller bound, because if it did, it would disagree with itself. A consequence of the theorem is that P is strictly contained in EXPTIME. So somewhere along the chain from P through NP through PSPACE to EXPTIME, at least one of the containments must be strict. We know that the chain grows; we just do not know where.

We have painted a bleak picture for anyone who needs to solve an NP-hard problem, but the picture in practice is far brighter than the theory alone would suggest. NP-hardness is a statement about worst case exact solutions in polynomial time. Giving up any one of these three requirements, the worst case, the exactness, or the polynomial bound, opens the door to useful methods. We describe the most important of these in turn.

The first strategy is approximation, which gives up on exactness. Many NP-hard problems are optimization problems, where we seek the smallest vertex cover or the shortest tour, and in such settings we may be content with a solution that is provably close to the best. An approximation algorithm is a polynomial time algorithm that always returns a solution whose value is within some guaranteed factor of the optimal value. For vertex cover, a simple greedy algorithm that repeatedly picks an uncovered edge and adds both of its endpoints returns a cover at most twice the size of the smallest one. For the traveling salesman problem with distances that satisfy the triangle inequality, there is an algorithm that returns a tour at most one and a half times the shortest. Recent work has improved this factor by a tiny amount. For some problems, such as knapsack, there are approximation schemes that come within any desired factor of the optimum, with running time that grows as the desired factor shrinks. For other problems, such as the general traveling salesman problem without the triangle inequality, no constant factor approximation is possible unless P equals NP. The theory of approximation thus has its own fine structure, with its own hardness results.

The second strategy is heuristics, which give up on the worst case. A heuristic is a method that works well on the instances one actually encounters but offers no guarantee of running time or solution quality. Modern SAT solvers are the outstanding example. Satisfiability is the original NP-complete problem, yet solvers based on clause learning and clever backtracking routinely solve instances with millions of variables that arise in hardware verification and software testing. These solvers are exponential in the worst case, and one can construct instances that defeat them, but the instances that arise in practice usually have structure that the solver can exploit.

The third strategy is parameterized algorithms, which refine the polynomial bound. Here one identifies a parameter of the instance, call it k, that is small in practice even when the instance size n is large. One then seeks an algorithm whose running time is exponential only in k and polynomial in n. For vertex cover, there is an algorithm that runs in time about two to the power of k times n. This is entirely practical when k is in the dozens even if n is in the millions. The problem is said to be fixed parameter tractable. Not every NP-hard problem has this property for every natural parameter; graph coloring with k colors, for example, is already NP-hard for k equal to three, so the number of colors is a useless parameter. Parameterized complexity has developed its own hierarchy of classes and its own hardness theory to sort out which parameters help and which do not.

The fourth strategy is to look for special cases. An NP-hard problem on general inputs may be easy on inputs with restricted structure. Many graph problems that are NP-hard on general graphs are in P on trees, on planar graphs, on graphs of bounded tree width, or on interval graphs. Subset sum is NP-hard in general, but there is a dynamic programming algorithm that runs in time proportional to the number of items times the target value. This is efficient when the target is small. Such an algorithm is called pseudo-polynomial, and it is genuinely useful even though it is not polynomial in the input size. Two-satisfiability, where every clause has two literals, is in P, even though three-satisfiability is NP-complete. The lesson is that NP-hardness is a property of a problem as stated, and restating the problem to reflect the actual instances one cares about can make all the difference.

We close this section with a survey of what remains unknown, since the open questions define the field as much as the theorems do. The central question is whether P equals NP. Closely related is whether NP equals coNP, and whether the polynomial hierarchy is infinite. Whether P equals PSPACE is open, as is whether NP equals PSPACE. Whether randomized polynomial time equals P is open, though recent results suggest that the answer is yes. Whether there are natural problems in NP that are neither in P nor NP-complete is open. A theorem of Ladner shows that if P does not equal NP then such intermediate problems must exist; graph isomorphism and factoring are the leading candidates. Whether quantum computers can solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time is open, though the evidence suggests they cannot, even though they can factor integers in polynomial time.

The deeper mystery is why the questions are so hard. Complexity theory has succeeded brilliantly at organizing problems into classes and at showing that the classes are tightly linked, so that resolving one question would resolve many others. It has succeeded less well at separating the classes. The barriers of relativization, natural proofs, and algebrization tell us that the obvious tools are not enough and that any proof will require a genuinely new idea. Perhaps that idea will come from algebra, or from geometry, or from the study of proof systems themselves. Perhaps it will show that P equals NP after all, and that the universe is far more computationally generous than we have assumed. Until then, the theory offers a map of the territory with one enormous blank region at its center. The problems we meet in practice are labeled by which side of the region we believe them to lie on. The next section of this book turns from the limits of computation in general to the specific models of computation that underlie modern artificial intelligence.


Every algorithm is a solution to a problem, but the solutions that survive in textbooks tend to share a small number of recurring shapes. These shapes are called algorithm design paradigms, and learning them is the closest thing computer science has to a general method for inventing new algorithms. A paradigm is not a single algorithm; it is a strategy for breaking a problem into subproblems, solving those subproblems, and combining their solutions into a solution for the original problem. This section surveys the major paradigms: divide and conquer, greedy algorithms, dynamic programming, the classical graph algorithms, randomized algorithms, and approximation algorithms. Along the way we will pay close attention to a question that is easy to neglect: when is a given paradigm actually correct for a given problem, and how do we prove it? We close by considering how an experienced designer chooses a paradigm when facing a problem that nobody has solved before.

The first paradigm, divide and conquer, is the one most students meet first, and it is the one with the cleanest analysis. The idea is to split the input into smaller pieces, solve each piece recursively, and then combine the piece solutions into a solution for the whole input. The recursion bottoms out when the input is so small that the problem can be solved directly. The running time of such an algorithm is described by a recurrence, an equation that expresses the time on an input of size n in terms of the time on smaller inputs plus the cost of splitting and combining. Solving the recurrence tells us the time complexity of the algorithm, and the master theorem gives a ready answer for the most common recurrence shapes.

Merge sort is the canonical example. To sort an array of n values, split the array into two halves, sort each half recursively, and then merge the two sorted halves into a single sorted array. Merging two sorted lists takes linear time in their combined length, because we walk through both lists once, always taking the smaller front value. The recurrence says that the time on n values equals twice the time on n over 2 values plus big O of n for the merge. The recursion tree has a depth of log n, and every level of the tree does a total of big O of n work, so the total time is big O of n log n. Merge sort is stable and its running time does not depend on the arrangement of the input, which makes it a safe default, but it needs extra memory for the merge step.

Quicksort divides the input in a different way. Instead of splitting the array in the middle, it chooses one value as a pivot, partitions the array so that every value smaller than the pivot sits on the left and every value larger sits on the right, and then recursively sorts the two sides. The partition step takes linear time and can be done in place, so quicksort uses very little extra memory. The catch is that the quality of the split depends on the pivot. If the pivot always lands near the median, the recurrence looks like merge sort and the time is big O of n log n. If the pivot is always the smallest or largest value, one side is empty and the other side has n minus 1 values, and the time degrades to big O of n squared. We will return to this weakness when we discuss randomized algorithms, because randomization is exactly the tool that fixes it.

Strassen's algorithm for matrix multiplication shows that divide and conquer can improve on an algorithm that seems hard to beat. Multiplying two n by n matrices by the definition takes big O of n cubed time, because each of the n squared output values is a sum of n products. If we split each matrix into four blocks of size n over 2, the natural recursive algorithm computes eight products of half-size matrices and combines them with additions. The recurrence for this approach gives big O of n cubed again, so splitting alone buys nothing. Strassen observed that with a clever set of additions and subtractions, the eight products can be replaced by seven. Seven recursive products of half-size matrices plus a quadratic amount of addition gives a recurrence whose solution is big O of n to the power log base 2 of 7, which is roughly n to the 2.81. The improvement comes entirely from reducing the number of subproblems, and this lesson generalizes: in divide and conquer, the number of recursive calls often matters more than the cost of combining.

The second paradigm is the greedy method. A greedy algorithm builds a solution one step at a time, and at every step it makes the choice that looks best right now, without considering how that choice might constrain future choices. Greedy algorithms are typically fast and easy to implement, which is both their attraction and their danger. They are attractive because when they work, they are usually the simplest possible solution. They are dangerous because they are often wrong, and a wrong greedy algorithm can look convincing until an adversarial input exposes it. The central skill with greedy algorithms is therefore not writing them but proving them correct.

Two properties make a greedy algorithm correct for a problem. The first is the greedy choice property: there exists an optimal solution that agrees with the greedy choice at the first step. The second is optimal substructure: after making the greedy choice, what remains is a smaller instance of the same problem, and an optimal solution to the original problem contains an optimal solution to that smaller instance. When both properties hold, induction on the number of steps shows that the greedy algorithm produces an optimal solution. The usual proof technique is an exchange argument. We take any optimal solution, we show that we can swap its first choice for the greedy choice without making the solution worse, and we repeat until the optimal solution has been transformed into the greedy one.

Interval scheduling is the cleanest illustration. We are given a set of intervals on a line, each with a start time and a finish time, and we want to select as many pairwise non-overlapping intervals as possible. Several greedy rules suggest themselves. We might take the shortest interval first, or the interval that starts earliest, or the interval that conflicts with the fewest others. Each of these rules fails on some input. The rule that works is to take the interval that finishes earliest, discard everything that overlaps it, and repeat. The exchange argument is straightforward: whatever interval an optimal solution picks first, the earliest-finishing interval ends no later, so we can swap it in without creating a conflict with the rest of the optimal solution. After sorting the intervals by finish time, the algorithm runs in big O of n log n time.

Huffman coding is a greedy algorithm for data compression. Given a set of symbols with known frequencies, we want a binary prefix code, meaning a code in which no codeword is a prefix of another, that minimizes the total length of the encoded message. Huffman's algorithm maintains a collection of trees, one for each symbol initially, each labeled with its frequency. At every step it removes the two trees with the smallest frequencies, joins them under a new root whose frequency is their sum, and puts the new tree back. When one tree remains, the path from the root to each symbol gives its codeword. The greedy choice property holds because the two least frequent symbols can always be made siblings at the deepest level of some optimal tree, and the substructure property holds because merging them produces a smaller instance of the same problem. With a priority queue to find the smallest frequencies, the algorithm takes big O of n log n time.

The minimum spanning tree problem is the most important greedy success story in graph theory. Given a connected graph with a weight on each edge, a spanning tree is a subset of edges that connects every vertex without forming a cycle, and we want the spanning tree whose total weight is smallest. The key fact that makes greedy algorithms work here is the cut property. A cut is a partition of the vertex set into two nonempty parts. The cut property says that for any cut, the lightest edge crossing the cut belongs to some minimum spanning tree. This single property justifies two different greedy algorithms that make the lightest-edge choice in different orders.

Kruskal's algorithm sorts all edges by weight and considers them from lightest to heaviest. It adds an edge to the growing forest if and only if the edge connects two vertices that are currently in different components; otherwise the edge would create a cycle and is discarded. The cut property applies because any edge that joins two components is the lightest edge crossing the cut between one of those components and everything else. To test quickly whether two vertices are in the same component, Kruskal's algorithm uses a union-find data structure, which supports both the test and the merge in nearly constant amortized time. The sorting step dominates, so the total time is big O of m log m for a graph with m edges, which is the same as big O of m log n since m is at most n squared.

Prim's algorithm grows a single tree from a starting vertex instead of a forest. At every step it looks at all edges with exactly one endpoint inside the tree and adds the lightest one, bringing a new vertex into the tree. The cut here is the tree versus the rest of the graph, and again the cut property guarantees that the chosen edge is safe. A priority queue holds the vertices outside the tree, keyed by the weight of the lightest edge connecting each to the tree. With a binary heap the running time is big O of m log n, and with a Fibonacci heap it improves to big O of m plus n log n. Prim's algorithm and Dijkstra's algorithm, which we meet later, have almost identical structure, differing only in what value the priority queue uses as a key.

The third paradigm, dynamic programming, is the one that students find hardest and that practitioners find most useful. Dynamic programming applies to problems that have optimal substructure, like greedy problems, but that lack the greedy choice property. In such problems we cannot know which first choice is right without exploring the consequences of every candidate choice. A naive recursive algorithm that explores all choices typically takes exponential time, because it solves the same subproblem over and over again. Dynamic programming observes that if the number of distinct subproblems is small, usually polynomial in the input size, then we can solve each subproblem once, store its solution in a table, and look it up whenever it is needed again. This property is called overlapping subproblems, and together with optimal substructure it is the signature of a dynamic programming problem.

There are two ways to organize the table. Memoization keeps the natural recursive structure of the algorithm but adds a cache. Before solving a subproblem, the algorithm checks whether the answer is already in the table; if it is, it returns the stored value, and if not, it computes the value, stores it, and returns it. Tabulation instead abandons recursion and fills the table iteratively, from the smallest subproblems to the largest, in an order that guarantees every subproblem's dependencies are already filled when it is reached. Memoization is easier to write and only solves the subproblems that are actually needed, while tabulation avoids the overhead of recursion and often allows the table to be compressed to a few rows when each subproblem depends only on recent ones. Both approaches produce the same answers in the same asymptotic time.

The Fibonacci numbers give the simplest possible demonstration. The nth Fibonacci number is the sum of the two preceding ones, with the first two equal to 1. The direct recursive algorithm makes two recursive calls for every call, so the number of calls grows exponentially in n, even though there are only n distinct subproblems. Memoization reduces the time to big O of n because each of the n subproblems is computed once. Tabulation fills an array from the bottom up in big O of n time, and since each value depends only on the two previous values, the array can be replaced by two variables, giving constant space. The lesson is not about Fibonacci numbers, which have a closed form anyway; the lesson is that a small number of overlapping subproblems can hide inside an exponential recursion.

The longest common subsequence problem shows dynamic programming on two sequences. A subsequence is obtained from a sequence by deleting zero or more symbols without reordering the rest. Given two sequences of lengths m and n, we want the longest sequence that is a subsequence of both. Define a subproblem for each pair of prefixes: the length of the longest common subsequence of the first i symbols of one sequence and the first j symbols of the other. If the last symbols of the two prefixes match, the answer is one plus the answer for the prefixes with both last symbols removed. If they do not match, the answer is the larger of the two answers obtained by removing the last symbol from one prefix or the other. The table has m plus 1 rows and n plus 1 columns, each cell is filled in constant time from its neighbors above and to the left, and the total time is big O of m times n. Walking backward through the table from the final cell recovers the actual subsequence, not just its length.

The knapsack problem introduces a subtlety about what counts as polynomial time. We have n items, each with a weight and a value, and a knapsack with a weight capacity. We want the subset of items with the largest total value whose total weight does not exceed the capacity. The subproblems are indexed by the number of items considered so far and the remaining capacity. For each item, we either leave it out, in which case the value is the same as for the previous item with the same capacity, or we put it in, in which case the value is the item's value plus the best value for the previous item with capacity reduced by the item's weight. The table has n rows and capacity plus 1 columns, so the time is big O of n times the capacity. This looks polynomial, but the capacity is a number written in the input, and a number with k digits can be as large as 10 to the k. The running time is therefore exponential in the length of the input, and this kind of running time is called pseudo-polynomial. The knapsack problem is NP-hard in general, and the dynamic programming algorithm is efficient only when the capacity is small.

Edit distance, also called Levenshtein distance, measures how different two strings are by counting the minimum number of insertions, deletions, and substitutions needed to turn one string into the other. The structure is almost identical to the longest common subsequence. The subproblem for a pair of prefixes asks for the edit distance between them. If the last symbols match, the distance equals the distance for the prefixes with the last symbols removed. Otherwise it is one plus the minimum of three options: the distance after deleting the last symbol from the first prefix, the distance after deleting the last symbol from the second prefix, and the distance after substituting one last symbol for the other, which removes both. The table again has m plus 1 by n plus 1 cells and takes big O of m times n time. Edit distance underlies spelling correction, DNA sequence alignment, and the diff tools that compare versions of a file, and all of these are the same dynamic programming algorithm with different costs on the three operations.

Graph algorithms form the fourth body of material, and they are less a single paradigm than a collection of techniques that every designer must know by heart. A graph is a set of vertices connected by edges, and an enormous number of problems can be modeled as questions about graphs: finding routes, scheduling tasks with dependencies, detecting cycles, allocating resources, and matching supply to demand. The two basic ways to explore a graph are breadth-first search and depth-first search, and almost every other graph algorithm is built on one of them.

Breadth-first search starts from a source vertex and explores the graph in layers. It first visits every vertex adjacent to the source, then every unvisited vertex adjacent to those, and so on, using a queue to hold the vertices whose neighbors have not yet been examined. Each vertex is placed in the queue once and each edge is examined once, so the time is big O of n plus m for a graph with n vertices and m edges. The layer in which a vertex is discovered equals its distance from the source measured in number of edges, so breadth-first search solves the shortest path problem in unweighted graphs. It also finds connected components and tests whether a graph is bipartite.

Depth-first search explores the graph by going as deep as possible along each branch before backtracking. It is naturally expressed recursively: visit a vertex, mark it, and then recursively visit each unmarked neighbor. The time is again big O of n plus m. Depth-first search records for every vertex the time at which it was first discovered and the time at which all its descendants were finished, and these timestamps carry a surprising amount of information. They classify every edge as a tree edge, a back edge, a forward edge, or a cross edge, and the presence of a back edge is exactly the condition for the graph to contain a cycle. Depth-first search is the engine behind algorithms for strongly connected components, articulation points, and bridges.

Topological sort is the first important application. A directed acyclic graph models a set of tasks with precedence constraints, where an edge from one vertex to another means the first task must finish before the second begins. A topological order is a listing of the vertices in which every edge points forward. Depth-first search produces one directly: run the search from every unvisited vertex, and output the vertices in decreasing order of their finishing times. The reason this works is that when a vertex finishes, every vertex reachable from it has already finished, so every vertex it points to appears later in the output. An alternative algorithm repeatedly removes a vertex with no incoming edges, maintaining a count of incoming edges for every vertex. Both approaches take big O of n plus m time, and both can detect that no topological order exists, which happens exactly when the graph contains a cycle.

Shortest paths in weighted graphs require more machinery. Dijkstra's algorithm computes the shortest path from a single source to every other vertex when all edge weights are nonnegative. It maintains a tentative distance for every vertex, initially zero for the source and infinite for everything else, and a priority queue keyed by tentative distance. At every step it removes the vertex with the smallest tentative distance, declares that distance final, and relaxes every outgoing edge, meaning it checks whether the path through the removed vertex gives a shorter tentative distance to the neighbor and updates if so. The correctness argument is greedy: when a vertex is removed, no shorter path to it can exist, because any such path would have to leave the set of finished vertices through an edge of nonnegative weight to some vertex with a larger tentative distance. With a binary heap the time is big O of m log n. The nonnegativity assumption is essential, and a single negative edge can make Dijkstra's algorithm return wrong answers.

The Bellman-Ford algorithm handles negative edge weights at the cost of a slower running time. It also maintains tentative distances, but instead of a priority queue it simply relaxes every edge in the graph, and it repeats this full pass n minus 1 times. After k passes, every shortest path using at most k edges has been found, and since a shortest path never needs more than n minus 1 edges when there are no negative cycles, n minus 1 passes suffice. The time is big O of n times m. One more pass reveals whether a negative cycle exists: if any edge can still be relaxed, some vertex is reachable from a negative cycle and no shortest path to it is defined. The structure of Bellman-Ford is really dynamic programming, with subproblems indexed by the number of edges allowed, and this viewpoint explains why it tolerates negative weights where the greedy Dijkstra does not.

Floyd-Warshall computes shortest paths between every pair of vertices at once. It is a pure dynamic programming algorithm whose subproblems are indexed by a pair of endpoints and by the set of vertices allowed as intermediate stops on the path. Number the vertices from 1 to n and let the kth stage allow only vertices numbered at most k as intermediates. The shortest path from one vertex to another using intermediates from the first k vertices either avoids vertex k, in which case it is the answer from stage k minus 1, or passes through vertex k exactly once, in which case it is the sum of the stage k minus 1 answers from the start to k and from k to the end. The table is updated in place with three nested loops, giving big O of n cubed time and big O of n squared space. For dense graphs this matches running Dijkstra from every vertex, and it is much simpler to implement and handles negative edges without modification.

Maximum flow asks a different kind of question. We have a directed graph with a capacity on each edge, a source vertex, and a sink vertex, and we want to send as much flow as possible from the source to the sink without exceeding any edge capacity and while conserving flow at every intermediate vertex. The Ford-Fulkerson method repeatedly finds an augmenting path from source to sink in the residual graph, which records how much additional flow each edge can carry and also includes reverse edges that allow previously sent flow to be cancelled. It pushes as much flow as the path allows and updates the residual graph. When no augmenting path remains, the flow is maximum. The proof is the max-flow min-cut theorem, which states that the value of the maximum flow equals the capacity of the minimum cut separating source from sink. Choosing augmenting paths by breadth-first search, which gives the Edmonds-Karp algorithm, guarantees big O of n times m squared time. Maximum flow is a remarkably versatile tool, because bipartite matching, edge-disjoint paths, and many scheduling problems reduce to it.

The fifth paradigm introduces randomness into the algorithm itself. A randomized algorithm makes random choices during its execution, and its running time or its output, or both, are random variables. This might seem like a strange thing to want, since we usually prefer predictable behavior, but randomness turns out to be a powerful resource. It can eliminate bad cases that depend on the input, it can produce simple algorithms where deterministic alternatives are complicated, and for some problems it gives faster algorithms than any known deterministic approach.

Randomized quicksort is the example that completes our earlier discussion. The weakness of quicksort is that a fixed pivot rule, such as always choosing the first value, has a bad input that forces quadratic time, and such inputs can arise naturally, for example when the input is already sorted. Randomized quicksort chooses the pivot uniformly at random from the current subarray. Now no input is bad; for every input, the expected running time over the algorithm's own random choices is big O of n log n. The analysis counts the expected number of comparisons by noting that two values are compared only if one of them is the first pivot chosen from the range of values between them, and summing these probabilities gives the result. The worst case is still quadratic, but it is reached only through bad luck in the coin flips, not through bad input, and the probability of being much slower than expected is vanishingly small.

Hashing is the other everyday use of randomization. A hash table stores keys in an array by computing a hash function that maps each key to an array index. If many keys collide at the same index, operations slow down. For any fixed hash function there is a set of keys that all collide, so an adversary who knows the function can force bad performance. Universal hashing picks the hash function at random from a carefully designed family, so that for any two distinct keys, the probability of a collision is at most one over the table size. The expected number of keys at any index is then constant when the table is sized proportionally to the number of keys, and lookups take expected constant time regardless of which keys are stored. As with quicksort, the randomness is in the algorithm rather than in the input, and the guarantee holds for every input.

Randomized algorithms are classified by what the randomness affects. A Las Vegas algorithm always produces a correct answer, but its running time is random. Randomized quicksort is a Las Vegas algorithm: the output is always sorted, and only the time varies. A Monte Carlo algorithm always runs within a fixed time bound, but its answer may be wrong with some small probability. The classic example is primality testing, where a Monte Carlo test picks a random witness and performs a computation that always reports prime when the input is prime but reports prime for a composite input with probability at most one quarter. Repeating the test k times with independent witnesses drives the error probability down to one over 4 to the k. A Monte Carlo algorithm can be converted into a Las Vegas algorithm when there is an efficient way to verify its answer, by simply repeating until the answer verifies, and a Las Vegas algorithm can be converted into a Monte Carlo algorithm by stopping after a fixed time and returning whatever is available.

The sixth paradigm, approximation, addresses the many optimization problems that are NP-hard and therefore unlikely to have exact polynomial time algorithms. Rather than give up, we lower our expectations from an optimal solution to a solution that is provably close to optimal. An approximation algorithm for a minimization problem has an approximation ratio of c if, on every input, the value of the solution it produces is at most c times the value of the optimal solution. For a maximization problem the solution value must be at least the optimal value divided by c. The crucial word is every: the guarantee must hold for all inputs, not just typical ones, and proving it usually requires finding a lower bound on the optimal value that the algorithm can be compared against.

Vertex cover gives a simple example with ratio 2. A vertex cover is a set of vertices that touches every edge in the graph, and we want the smallest one. The algorithm repeatedly picks any uncovered edge, adds both of its endpoints to the cover, and removes every edge touching either endpoint. The edges picked by the algorithm share no endpoints, so any vertex cover, including the optimal one, must contain at least one endpoint of each. The algorithm's cover has exactly two vertices per picked edge, so it is at most twice the size of the optimal cover. The algorithm is greedy in flavor, and the proof follows the pattern of bounding the optimal value from below by something the algorithm's solution is bounded above by.

The traveling salesman problem on metric instances, where distances satisfy the triangle inequality, has a well known 2-approximation based on minimum spanning trees. Compute a minimum spanning tree, walk around it visiting every vertex, and shortcut the walk so that each vertex is visited once. The tree weight is a lower bound on the optimal tour, because deleting any edge from a tour gives a spanning tree. The walk around the tree has twice the tree weight, and the triangle inequality guarantees that shortcuts never increase the length. Christofides' algorithm improves the ratio to three halves by adding a minimum weight perfect matching on the odd degree vertices of the tree instead of doubling every edge. Without the triangle inequality, no constant ratio is possible unless P equals NP, which illustrates that the achievable approximation ratio is itself a property of the problem worth studying.

We have now surveyed the paradigms, and we can turn to the question that motivates the survey: faced with a new problem, how does one choose? There is no algorithm for designing algorithms, but there is a disciplined way of thinking that experienced designers follow, and it can be described as a sequence of questions to ask about the problem.

The first question is whether the problem is already a known problem in disguise. A startling fraction of problems encountered in practice are shortest paths, maximum flow, minimum spanning tree, sorting, or longest common subsequence wearing unfamiliar clothing. Before designing anything, try to model the problem as a graph, a sequence, or a set of intervals, and ask whether one of the classical algorithms applies directly. Reductions of this kind are the most valuable skill in the whole subject, because they replace design with recognition.

The second question is whether the problem decomposes into independent pieces. If an input can be split into parts such that the parts can be solved separately and the results combined cheaply, divide and conquer is the natural candidate. The warning sign that it will work is that the combination step is simpler than the original problem, as merging is simpler than sorting. The warning sign that it will not work is that the pieces interact, so that the solution to one piece depends on the solution to another; in that case the subproblems overlap, and dynamic programming is the better fit.

The third question is whether a locally best choice can be made safely. Try to formulate a greedy rule and then try hard to break it with a small counterexample. If every rule you try fails, the problem probably lacks the greedy choice property and needs dynamic programming or something stronger. If a rule survives all your attempts, try to prove it with an exchange argument. Greedy algorithms should be adopted only with a proof in hand, because the ones that fail do so silently on inputs you did not think of.

The fourth question is whether the problem has optimal substructure with overlapping subproblems. Write down the brute force recursive algorithm that tries every choice, and count the distinct subproblems it encounters. If the count is polynomial, even though the recursion tree is exponential, dynamic programming will convert the exponential algorithm into a polynomial one. The art is in choosing the subproblem definition, and the common patterns are prefixes, suffixes, contiguous intervals, and pairs of prefixes. If no polynomial subproblem space can be found, the problem may be NP-hard, and it is time to search for a known hardness result.

The fifth question is whether the problem is hard, and if so, what kind of answer is acceptable. If the problem is NP-hard and the inputs are small, an exponential algorithm with good pruning may be perfectly adequate. If the inputs are large and an optimal answer is not required, an approximation algorithm with a proven ratio is the principled choice, and a greedy rule is often where to look first. If the numbers in the input are small, a pseudo-polynomial dynamic programming algorithm may run fast in practice despite its theoretical exponential bound.


This final section gathers together several frontiers of computer science theory and asks how they rest on the foundations built in the earlier sections. Each topic here could fill a book of its own, so the goal is not completeness but orientation. We want to see how information, computation, proof, and physical machines connect into one picture. The thread running through everything is the humble bit: a single unit of information that can take one of two values. From that unit we build messages, programs, machines, and proofs, and we eventually run into the limits of what any machine can do.

We begin with information theory, which asks a question that sounds simple but turns out to be deep: how much information does a message carry? The answer, worked out by Claude Shannon in the middle of the twentieth century, is that information is a measure of surprise. A message that tells you something you already expected carries little information, while a message that tells you something unlikely carries a great deal. Suppose a source produces one of several possible messages, each with some probability. The information in a particular message is then the log base two of one over its probability. A message with probability one half carries one bit of information, a message with probability one quarter carries two bits, and so on. The average information per message over the whole source is called the entropy of the source.

Entropy is measured in bits, and this is no accident. The entropy of a source tells you the smallest average number of bits you need to represent one message from that source. This is the heart of what is called the source coding theorem. If a source has entropy of three bits per message, then no scheme for encoding messages can average fewer than three bits per message over the long run. Conversely, there exist schemes that come as close to three bits per message as you like. Entropy is therefore both a lower bound and an achievable target for compression.

Consider a source that produces four possible messages, each with probability one quarter. The entropy is two bits per message, and indeed two bits suffice to label four things. Now suppose instead that one message has probability one half, another has probability one quarter, and the remaining two each have probability one eighth. The entropy is now one and three quarters bits per message. A clever code assigns a one-bit codeword to the most common message, a two-bit codeword to the next, and three-bit codewords to the rare ones. The average length comes out to exactly one and three quarters bits, matching the entropy. The lesson is that compression works by giving short codes to frequent messages and long codes to rare ones.

Information theory also asks what happens when a message passes through a noisy channel that may flip some bits. One might expect that any noise at all makes reliable communication impossible, or that reliability can only be bought by sending the message many times over. Shannon showed something surprising: every noisy channel has a capacity, measured in bits per use of the channel. Below that capacity one can communicate with an error rate as small as desired. The method is to add carefully structured redundancy to the message. This structured redundancy is the subject of the theory of error-correcting codes.

The simplest error-correcting idea is repetition: send each bit three times and take the majority on the receiving end. This corrects any single flipped bit in each group of three, but it costs a factor of three in length. Better codes add fewer redundant bits yet correct more errors. The key concept is distance: a code is a set of allowed codewords, and the distance between two codewords is the number of positions where they differ. If every pair of codewords differs in at least three positions, then any single error leaves the received word closer to the original codeword than to any other. The receiver can then recover the message by choosing the nearest codeword. A code with minimum distance of two t plus one corrects up to t errors. Designing codes that have large distance while wasting few bits is a rich mathematical problem. Such codes sit inside every disk drive, every memory chip, and every wireless link in use today.

The deeper point is that information theory gives a precise meaning to the word information, independent of what the message is about. A bit is a bit whether it encodes a letter, a pixel, or a measurement. This abstraction is what allows the same compression and error correction methods to apply to every kind of data. It also connects back to computation, because the bits that represent a message are the very same bits a machine reads and writes. Computation is the transformation of information, and information theory tells us how much there is to transform.

We turn next to cryptography, which asks how to protect information from those who should not have it. For most of history, cryptography was a craft rather than a science, and codes were broken as fast as they were invented. The theory of computation changed this by giving cryptography a foundation: the idea that some problems are hard to solve even though their solutions are easy to check. This is exactly the distinction between the class P and the class NP that we studied earlier, and modern cryptography is built on the assumption that the two classes differ.

The central object in this foundation is the one-way function. A one-way function is a function that is easy to compute in one direction but hard to invert. Given an input, a machine can compute the output in polynomial time. Given an output, no efficient machine can find an input that produces it, except with negligible probability. Multiplying two large prime numbers is believed to be such a function, since the product is easy to compute. Recovering the primes from the product is the factoring problem, for which no efficient classical method is known. Note carefully the word believed in the previous sentence, because it carries real weight. Nobody has proved that factoring is hard, and nobody has proved that any one-way function exists at all. If one-way functions exist, then P is not equal to NP, so a proof of their existence would settle the greatest open problem in the field.

This is why hardness assumptions matter so much in the theory of cryptography. Every practical cryptographic system is secure only relative to an assumption that some specific problem is hard. If the assumption is false, the system is broken, no matter how carefully it was built. Cryptographers therefore try to base systems on problems that have resisted attack for a long time. They also try to prove that breaking the system would require solving the hard problem. A proof of this form is called a reduction, the same tool used in complexity theory to show that one problem is at least as hard as another. The system is then exactly as secure as the underlying problem is hard, and no more.

The most striking idea in modern cryptography is the public key. In older systems, two parties had to share a secret key in advance. The problem of delivering that key securely was as hard as the original problem of communicating securely. In a public key system, each party has two keys: a public key that anyone may see, and a private key that is kept secret. A message encrypted with the public key can only be decrypted with the private key. Anyone can send a secret message to a party by using that party's public key, yet only the holder of the private key can read it.

The reason this works is a one-way function with a trapdoor. The function is easy to compute and hard to invert, just as before, but there is a piece of secret information, the trapdoor, that makes inversion easy. In the system based on factoring, the public key includes the product of two large primes, and the private key includes the primes themselves. Computing with the product is easy, and undoing that computation is hard unless you know the factors. The security of the system reduces to the hardness of factoring, together with some additional assumptions about the specific operations involved.

Public key ideas extend well beyond the problem of secrecy. A digital signature is a value that only the holder of a private key could have produced, but which anyone with the public key can verify. This turns the public key system around: the private key is used to produce the signature, and the public key is used to check it. Signatures give us authentication, the assurance that a message came from a particular party and was not altered. Together, encryption and signatures are the foundation of every secure connection on the internet.

One more point about hardness assumptions deserves emphasis before we move on. Because cryptographic security rests on computational hardness, any change in what is computationally feasible changes what is secure. A faster classical method for factoring would weaken systems built on factoring. A new kind of machine that solves factoring efficiently would break them entirely. As we shall see when we reach quantum computing, such a machine is at least theoretically possible. This fact has driven a search for new hardness assumptions that would survive it.

We now step back from the applications of computation to its foundations and look at a model of computation very different from the Turing machine. The lambda calculus was invented by Alonzo Church in the nineteen thirties, at the same time that Turing was describing his machines. It was designed to capture the notion of a computable function in the purest possible way. Where the Turing machine has a tape, a head, and a table of states, the lambda calculus has only three ingredients: variables, abstraction, and application. Everything else in the calculus is built from these three forms.

A variable is simply a name, a placeholder for some value that is not yet known. An abstraction builds a function from a variable and a body: it says, take the value named by this variable, and produce this body as the result. In words, the abstraction over x with body x plus one is the function that adds one to its argument. An application takes a function and an argument and puts them together: it says, apply this function to this value. The entire calculus consists of expressions built by nesting these three forms, and the entire notion of computation consists of one rule for simplifying them.

That single rule of simplification is known as beta reduction, and it is the engine of the calculus. When an abstraction is applied to an argument, the result is the body of the abstraction with every occurrence of the variable replaced by the argument. Applying the function that adds one to its argument to the value five yields five plus one, and the computation proceeds by replacing names with values. A computation in the lambda calculus is a sequence of such reductions, and it halts when no further reduction is possible. The final expression, if there is one, is the value of the original expression.

It may seem impossible that such a sparse system could express anything interesting, since there are no numbers, no truth values, and no data structures built in. Church showed that all of these can be defined as functions. The trick for numbers is to represent each number by the act of repeating a function that many times. The Church numeral for zero is the function that takes a function f and a value x and returns x unchanged, applying f zero times. The Church numeral for one takes f and x and returns f applied to x, applying f once. The Church numeral for three applies f three times to x. A number, in this encoding, is a function that knows how to iterate.

With numbers represented this way, arithmetic becomes a matter of combining iterations. To add two Church numerals, build a function that first iterates f the first number of times and then iterates it the second number of times. To multiply two numbers, iterate the iteration, repeating the first number's repetition the second number of times. Truth values can be encoded as functions that choose between two arguments: true picks the first and false picks the second. A conditional expression is then just an application of a truth value to two branches. Pairs, lists, and every other structure follow the same pattern. Recursion is achieved by a clever expression called a fixed point combinator that allows a function to refer to itself.

The deep result is that the lambda calculus and the Turing machine compute exactly the same functions. Every function that a Turing machine can compute can be expressed in the lambda calculus, and every lambda calculus expression can be evaluated by a Turing machine. This equivalence was one of the original pieces of evidence for the Church-Turing thesis, the claim that these models capture the intuitive notion of effective computation. Two models invented independently, with entirely different flavors, turned out to agree on what is computable. Every model of computation proposed since then has agreed as well.

The pure lambda calculus has one unsettling property: it allows expressions that reduce forever. An abstraction that applies its argument to itself, applied to itself, reduces to itself and never halts. This is the halting problem showing up in a new costume. It also allows expressions that make no sense, such as applying a number to a truth value. Type theory was developed to tame both problems, and it turns out to connect computation to logic in a way that nobody anticipated.

A type is a classification of values according to how they may be used. A value might have the type of natural numbers, or the type of truth values, or the type of functions from natural numbers to truth values. In a typed lambda calculus, every expression is assigned a type, and the rules only allow an abstraction to be applied to an argument of the right type. A function from numbers to numbers can only be applied to a number. Expressions that would make no sense are not merely wrong; they are not even well-formed, and the type checker rejects them before any computation happens.

Types also control termination, which addresses the second of the two problems. In the simplest typed lambda calculus, the one called the simply typed lambda calculus, every well-typed expression reduces to a value in a finite number of steps. The self-applying expression that runs forever cannot be given a type, because a function cannot be applied to itself without the type of the function containing itself as a part. This is a great gain in safety but a loss in power: the simply typed calculus cannot express every computable function, precisely because it cannot express nontermination. Richer type systems recover much of the lost power while keeping most of the safety, and the design of such systems is an active area of research.

The connection to logic is the Curry-Howard correspondence, named for Haskell Curry and William Howard, who noticed it independently. The observation is that the rules for assigning types to expressions look exactly like the rules for proving propositions in logic. A function type from A to B corresponds to the proposition that A implies B. A pair type corresponds to the conjunction of two propositions. A choice between two types corresponds to a disjunction of two propositions. Under this correspondence, a type is a proposition, and an expression of that type is a proof of that proposition.

Consider the function that takes an argument of type A and returns it unchanged. Its type is the function type from A to A. The corresponding proposition is that A implies A, and the identity function is a proof of it: given evidence for A, return that same evidence. Consider next the function that takes a pair and returns its first component. Its type is the function type from the pair of A and B to A, and the corresponding proposition is that A and B together imply A. The function that extracts the first component is the proof of this proposition. Every well-typed program is a proof of the proposition given by its type, and every constructive proof can be read as a program.

The correspondence runs much deeper than a mere analogy between two notations. Reducing an expression corresponds to simplifying a proof by removing detours. The fact that every well-typed expression in the simply typed calculus reduces to a value corresponds to the fact that every proof in that logic has a normal form. Computation and proof simplification are the same process seen from two sides. This is why the correspondence is sometimes stated as a slogan: propositions are types, proofs are programs, and simplification of proofs is evaluation of programs.

The correspondence also sheds light on the limits of computation discussed in earlier sections. A logic that could prove every true proposition would correspond to a type system in which every true type is inhabited by a program. Godel showed, however, that no sufficiently rich logic can prove all truths about arithmetic. The halting problem, the incompleteness theorems, and the impossibility of a perfect type system are three faces of the same limit. Whatever formal system we build, there will be true statements it cannot prove, programs it cannot verify, and computations it cannot predict.

Everything so far has assumed a single machine executing a single computation one step at a time. Real systems, however, rarely look anything like this simple picture. A modern processor runs many threads at once, a data center runs many processors, and a global service runs many data centers connected by unreliable networks. The theory of concurrency and distributed computing asks what changes when there is more than one actor. It also asks what can be guaranteed when the actors cannot fully trust the network or each other. The answers include some of the most striking impossibility results in the field.

The first difficulty appears as soon as two threads share state. Suppose two threads each want to add one to a shared counter. Each reads the current value, computes the value plus one, and writes the result back. If the threads run one after the other, the counter goes up by two. If they interleave, both may read the same old value, both compute the same new value, and both write it, so the counter goes up by only one. The final result depends on the exact timing of the steps, which is not under the program's control. This is a race condition, and it is the fundamental hazard of concurrent computation.

The classical remedy is mutual exclusion: arrange matters so that only one thread at a time may execute the critical section of code that touches the shared state. The region of code is protected by a lock, and a thread must acquire the lock before entering and release it on leaving. The hard part is implementing the lock itself using only the basic operations the machine provides. Early solutions used only reads and writes and were remarkably subtle. A naive attempt to check whether the lock is free and then take it is itself a race condition. Modern machines provide atomic operations that read and modify a value in a single indivisible step, and locks are built on these.

Mutual exclusion solves the race condition but introduces new problems of its own. If a thread holds one lock and waits for another, while a second thread holds the second lock and waits for the first, both wait forever. This situation is called deadlock, and the system makes no further progress. If a thread is repeatedly denied the lock because others always get there first, it may never make progress, even though the system as a whole is active. This situation is called starvation, and it is harder to detect than deadlock. A correct concurrent algorithm must guarantee safety, meaning that nothing bad happens, such as two threads in the critical section at once. It must also guarantee liveness, meaning that something good eventually happens, such as every waiting thread eventually entering. Reasoning about both properties at once, over all possible interleavings, is far harder than reasoning about a sequential program.

Distributed computing adds a new dimension: the actors are separate machines that communicate only by sending messages, and messages may be delayed or lost. There is no shared memory, no shared clock, and no global view of the system. A machine cannot tell the difference between a peer that has crashed and a peer whose messages are merely slow. This uncertainty is the source of most of the difficulty in the field. It is the reason that problems which are trivial on a single machine become deep on a network.

The central problem of the whole field of distributed computing is called consensus. A set of machines each start with a proposed value, and they must all decide on a single value that was proposed by at least one of them. Every machine that does not crash must eventually decide, no two machines may decide differently, and the decision must not be a value nobody proposed. Consensus is the foundation of nearly every distributed service in use today. Agreeing on the order of transactions, electing a leader, replicating a database, and committing a change across several sites all reduce to consensus. If consensus can be solved, almost everything can be built on top of it.

In nineteen eighty-five, Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson studied a purely asynchronous system, where message delays are finite but unbounded. They proved that no deterministic algorithm can solve consensus in such a system if even a single machine may crash. This is the FLP impossibility result, named for the initials of its three authors. The proof shows that for any proposed algorithm, there is some schedule of message deliveries that keeps the system undecided forever. The intuition is that a machine waiting for a message cannot know whether to keep waiting or to proceed without it. Whichever choice it makes, an adversary controlling the delays can arrange for that choice to be wrong. The result does not say that consensus is impossible in practice; it says that no algorithm can guarantee termination in all cases under these assumptions.

Practical systems escape the FLP result by weakening its assumptions. Some assume that the network is eventually synchronous, meaning that delays are bounded after some unknown point in time, and under this assumption consensus is achievable. Some use randomness, which the proof does not cover, and achieve consensus with probability one. Some accept that in rare adverse conditions the system may stall, and they design so that a stall is detected and the system recovers when conditions improve. The algorithms that result, with names such as Paxos and Raft, are at the heart of the infrastructure that runs the internet. Their correctness is argued with exactly the kind of case analysis over possible schedules that the FLP proof pioneered.

A related limit is captured by the CAP idea, which concerns a distributed data store. Consistency means that every read sees the most recent write. Availability means that every request eventually receives a response of some kind. Partition tolerance means that the system keeps working even when the network splits into pieces that cannot communicate. The CAP idea states that during a partition, a system must choose between consistency and availability. If one side of the partition accepts a write, the other side either returns stale data, giving up consistency, or refuses to answer, giving up availability. There is no third option available while the partition lasts.

The CAP idea is less a theorem than a design principle, but it is a useful one. It forces the designer of any distributed system to decide what happens when the network fails, because the network will fail. Some systems choose consistency and simply refuse to answer requests during partitions. Others choose availability and reconcile any conflicting writes after the partition heals. Neither choice is wrong; the choice depends on what the application can tolerate. The deeper lesson, shared with FLP, is that distributed computation is governed by impossibility results just as sequential computation is. Good engineering begins by knowing exactly which guarantees cannot be had.

We turn finally to a frontier that questions the physical basis of computation itself. Every model discussed so far, from the Turing machine to the lambda calculus, has assumed that the state of a machine is a definite configuration of bits. Quantum mechanics describes a world in which physical systems do not have definite states in this sense, and quantum computing asks what happens when we build machines that exploit this.

The basic unit of quantum information is the qubit, or quantum bit. A classical bit is either zero or one, and never anything in between. A qubit can be in a superposition of zero and one, a state described by two complex numbers called amplitudes, one for each possibility. When the qubit is measured, it yields zero or one, with probabilities given by the squares of the magnitudes of the amplitudes, and the superposition collapses to the measured value. Before measurement, however, operations on the qubit act on both amplitudes at once. A register of n qubits has two to the power of n amplitudes, one for every possible configuration of n classical bits. An operation on the register acts on all of these amplitudes simultaneously.

This sounds like free parallelism on an enormous scale, but it is not. The amplitudes cannot be read out directly, and measurement yields only one configuration. A quantum algorithm must arrange the operations so that amplitudes for wrong answers cancel each other out while amplitudes for right answers reinforce, a phenomenon called interference. Designing such cancellation is the whole art of quantum algorithms, and it works only for problems with special structure. Quantum computers are not simply faster classical computers; they are machines that can perform a different kind of computation.

Entanglement is the second resource that quantum computation draws upon. Two qubits are entangled when their joint state cannot be described as a separate state for each. Measuring one immediately determines the result of measuring the other, no matter how far apart they are. Entanglement is what makes a register of n qubits genuinely different from n separate qubits. It allows the two to the power of n amplitudes to be correlated in ways that a classical description cannot compactly represent. Without entanglement, a quantum computer can be simulated efficiently by a classical one, and the quantum advantage vanishes.

Two famous algorithms show why all of this matters in practice. In nineteen ninety-four, Peter Shor gave a quantum algorithm that factors an n-digit number in time polynomial in n. The best known classical algorithms take time that grows faster than any polynomial. Shor's algorithm works by converting factoring into the problem of finding the period of a certain function. It then uses interference across a superposition of all inputs to make that period show up in a measurement. Since the security of the most widely used public key systems rests on the hardness of factoring, a large quantum computer would break them. This is the connection promised earlier: a new kind of machine changes what is feasible, and therefore changes what is secure.

Grover's algorithm, from nineteen ninety-six, addresses a far more general problem: searching an unstructured collection of n items for one that satisfies some condition. Classically, this requires examining about n items in the worst case. Grover's algorithm finds the item using about the square root of n queries, by repeatedly amplifying the amplitude of the correct answer. The speedup is only quadratic rather than exponential, but it applies to any search problem at all, including the search for a satisfying assignment in an NP problem. It is also known to be optimal: no quantum algorithm can search an unstructured collection in fewer than about the square root of n queries.

Taken together, these two results sharpen our understanding of computational complexity. The class of problems efficiently solvable by a quantum computer, called BQP, contains P and contains factoring, but is not believed to contain all of NP. Grover's algorithm shows that quantum computers help with NP problems only by a square root, and the optimality result shows that this is the best possible for unstructured search. The question of exactly where BQP sits relative to the classical classes is open, like most questions in complexity theory. What is settled is that the Church-Turing thesis in its original form survives, since a quantum computer computes nothing that a Turing machine cannot. The stronger version about efficiency, however, is another matter entirely. That version claims that any reasonable machine can be simulated by a Turing machine with only polynomial slowdown, and it is now in genuine doubt.

We close by asking how the whole field fits together. The story begins with the bit, a single choice between two values. Information theory tells us that every message, however complex, is a collection of bits. It also tells us how many bits a message really contains and how to protect those bits from noise. The bit is not a property of any particular machine; it is an abstraction of choice itself. That is why it serves as the common currency of everything that follows.

Automata and formal languages show what can be done with bounded memory, and the Turing machine shows what can be done with unbounded memory. The lambda calculus shows that the same functions can be described with no memory at all, only functions applied to functions. The agreement of these models is the Church-Turing thesis, and it tells us that the notion of computation is robust: it does not depend on the details of any machine. Computability theory then shows that this robust notion has limits. The halting problem cannot be solved, and by Rice's theorem almost nothing interesting about the behavior of programs can be decided in general.

Complexity theory refines the question from what can be computed to what can be computed efficiently. The classes P and NP capture the difference between finding a solution and checking one. The open question of whether they are equal is the deepest question in the field. Cryptography takes the presumed difference and turns it into a resource, building secrecy out of hardness. Type theory connects computation back to logic, showing that programs are proofs and that the limits of proof and the limits of computation are the same limits. Concurrency and distributed computing show that when many machines compute together, new impossibilities appear, governed not by the halting problem but by the uncertainty of communication. Quantum computing shows that the physical substrate matters, at least for efficiency, and that the map of complexity classes must make room for machines that exploit superposition and interference.

What unites all of these topics is a single method of inquiry. In each case, a precise mathematical model is proposed for some intuitive notion: information, computation, proof, agreement, or physical state. The model is studied until it yields both constructions, showing what can be done, and impossibility results, showing what cannot. The constructions become the algorithms and systems we use every day. The impossibility results become the boundaries that every engineer must respect. Theoretical computer science is the discipline of finding where those boundaries lie. The history of the field is the history of discovering that the boundaries are often in surprising places.

The student who has followed this book from its first section to this one has seen that history compressed into a single argument. Bits can be measured, compressed, and protected from noise by mathematics alone. Machines can be classified precisely by the functions that they compute. Some problems cannot be computed at all, and among those that can, some cannot be computed quickly. Hardness can be turned into security, proof can be turned into programs, and agreement can be reached only when the network cooperates. Even the physical world imposes its own version of these rules. Every one of these facts was established by proof, and every proof rests on a model that someone first had the imagination to write down. That act of modeling, more than any particular theorem, is what the theory of computation has to teach.
